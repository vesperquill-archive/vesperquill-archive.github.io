Chapter 5

The ballroom had scarcely recovered from the spectacle when Varkas turned upon Talia. His hand remained firmly about her wrist, and the severity of his expression was such that even those who had gathered to enjoy the quarrel began to regard it with apprehension.

“You appear determined to make an enemy of every person who has ever shown you the slightest consideration,” he said. His voice was quiet, but there was nothing gentle in it. “Is there truly no injury you will consider too cruel to inflict?”

Talia attempted to free herself. The effort was useless. His hold possessed the unyielding strength of a man accustomed to wielding weapons, and she resented the ease with which he restrained her.

“Release me.”

“Not until I am satisfied that you intend no further harm.”

“How noble of you,” she replied, raising her chin. “Does defending your beloved princess make you feel particularly virtuous?”

Varkas's expression darkened.

“This has nothing to do with virtue. You have frightened and humiliated a woman who offered you nothing but courtesy.”

“And what a magnificent display of courtesy it was! I am certain the entire court will speak of her generosity for weeks.”

“You mistake patience for weakness.”

“And you mistake my conduct for the worst of which I am capable.”

For a moment, neither spoke. Talia looked directly into his eyes, determined that he should discover no trace of the anguish concealed beneath her defiance.

“You stand there as though you have measured every failing of my character,” she continued. “But you have scarcely begun to understand me, Lord Sierkhan. There are depths of bitterness to which even your imagination has never descended.”

“I have witnessed enough.”

“Then perhaps you ought to avert your eyes. I should hate to disappoint you by becoming something even more dreadful.”

She smiled, though the expression was scarcely recognisable as one of amusement. Varkas regarded her with an exhaustion more painful than anger.

It was that look which wounded her most deeply.

Had he shouted, she might have answered him. Had he struck her, she might have hated him without reservation. But his evident disgust left her with nothing against which to defend herself.

Despise me, then. If that is all you will ever offer, I shall give you every reason to continue.

The thought brought a strange calm to her face.

“Do not imagine,” she said, “that this evening has revealed everything I am capable of becoming.”

Varkas's fingers tightened briefly around her wrist.

Before he could reply, a trembling voice called his name.

“Varkas.”

He turned immediately.

Aila stood a little distance away, surrounded by attendants who appeared uncertain whether they ought to approach her. Her beautiful gown was ruined, and the distress upon her face had stripped away the composure she had maintained throughout the evening.

“I cannot remain here,” she whispered. “Will you please take me somewhere private?”

The change in Varkas was immediate. His anger did not disappear, but his attention moved wholly towards the woman who had appealed to him.

“Of course, Your Highness.”

He released Talia and went to Aila's side, offering his arm before gently guiding her away from the assembled company.

Talia watched them depart.

She had imagined that seeing Aila humiliated would bring her some measure of satisfaction. Instead, she discovered that the sight of Varkas attending so carefully to his fiancée had made the evening more unbearable than before.

He did not look back.

Not once.

The certainty of his indifference settled upon her with a weight that threatened to crush the last remnants of her composure.

Yet she would sooner have endured any torment than allow the court to witness her despair.

Accordingly, she lifted her head, arranged her lips into a faint smile, and turned towards the refreshments as though she had merely concluded a tiresome conversation.

The guests withdrew from her path with remarkable haste. Their expressions ranged from disapproval to outright alarm, but Talia found their fear almost amusing.

At least they no longer pretended to admire her.

She selected a fresh glass of wine and was preparing to drink when Count Serian appeared beside her.

He removed the glass from her hand.

“I believe,” he said, “that the evening has afforded you quite sufficient entertainment.”

Talia glanced at him.

“Are you suggesting that I have overstayed my welcome?”

“I am suggesting that remaining here would be exceedingly unwise.”

“How extraordinary. I had understood that this celebration was intended to provide pleasure to all its guests.”

“The invitation was not, I suspect, intended to include an attempt upon the bride's dignity.”

“Then the invitation ought to have been more specific.”

The Count did not smile.

His attention had shifted towards the opposite side of the ballroom, where Gares stood among a group of courtiers.

The Crown Prince's expression was terrible.

Even at that distance, Talia could discern the violence of his anger. His hands were clenched, and he appeared scarcely conscious of the gentlemen attempting to address him.

“Your brother,” the Count murmured, “is not a man whose temper I should care to test further tonight.”

Talia followed his gaze.

Under ordinary circumstances, the sight might have encouraged her to devise some fresh provocation. There was a peculiar pleasure in discovering how readily Gares could be driven beyond the limits of royal dignity.

But the evening had exhausted her.

The quarrel with Varkas had left an emptiness which no victory over her brother could relieve.

“Very well,” she said at last. “You may escort me.”

Count Serian offered his arm, and Talia accepted it with the graciousness expected of a princess. Together they crossed the ballroom, neither hurrying nor lingering, while the curious company watched their departure.

Only when the doors had closed behind them did Talia permit herself a long, weary breath.

Outside, the palace gardens lay beneath the evening darkness. A carriage had already been brought forward, its lamps casting pale circles upon the gravel.

The royal guard opened the door.

Talia gathered her skirts and stepped inside.

She had scarcely begun to settle herself when a violent movement behind her interrupted her progress. The next instant, she was thrown against the carriage floor.

Before she could understand what had happened, Gares appeared above her.

His face bore so little resemblance to the polished prince who received ambassadors and addressed the imperial court that, for one dreadful moment, Talia scarcely recognised him.

“You have mistaken our silence for helplessness,” he said.

Talia struggled to rise.

“Have you followed me merely to deliver another lecture?”

His hand closed about her throat.

The question died upon her lips.

For an instant she remained perfectly still, astonished less by the pain than by the sudden realisation that her brother intended to hurt her.

Then instinct overcame surprise.

She seized his wrist and attempted to wrench it away, but Gares possessed both the advantage of strength and the terrible determination of a man who had surrendered himself entirely to rage.

Beyond the carriage door, the guard shouted in alarm.

“Your Imperial Highness! I beg you—”

“Stand aside,” Gares commanded.

The guard faltered. To lay hands upon the heir to the throne was an offence no ordinary soldier could contemplate without terror.

Talia understood that hesitation with a clarity which made her hatred burn hotter.

Even now, surrounded by servants sworn to protect the imperial household, she could not be certain that anyone would dare intervene.

Gares bent nearer.

“For years,” he said, “we have endured every insult you and your mother have chosen to inflict upon this family.”

His voice trembled with fury.

“You parade through these halls as though your presence were a privilege bestowed upon us. You insult my sister, mock our mother, and imagine that no consequence will ever reach you.”

Talia tried to answer, but the pressure upon her throat made speech impossible.

She struck at his hands, her carefully tended nails digging into his skin.

He scarcely appeared to feel it.

“Understand this,” he continued. “Our restraint has never been affection. It has never been forgiveness. And it must not be mistaken for fear.”

The carriage seemed to grow smaller around her.

The air she struggled to draw into her lungs was painfully insufficient, and the edges of her vision began to darken.

At last, Gares released her.

Talia collapsed against the seat, coughing violently. She pressed both hands to her throat and struggled to recover her breath while her brother straightened his coat.

He regarded her with cold contempt.

“Whatever position you believe yourself to possess within this palace, remember that it is not beyond challenge.”

She looked up at him through watering eyes.

Gares paused at the carriage door.

“There will come a day when neither your mother's favour nor your father's indulgence will protect you.”

With that, he stepped outside and closed the door.

The sudden quiet was almost unbearable.

Talia remained where she had fallen, breathing with difficulty. Her throat ached, and each attempt to swallow brought fresh discomfort.

For several moments, she could do nothing but stare at the dim interior of the carriage.

Then her gaze fell upon her hands.

Two of her fingernails had broken.

She examined them with a curious detachment. The edges were jagged, and a little blood had gathered where the nails had torn.

She had spent so much time shaping them, taking an almost childish satisfaction in their elegant appearance.

Now they were ruined.

“How troublesome,” she whispered.

Her voice sounded strange to her own ears.

She touched one of the broken nails and winced.

“I shall have to begin again.”

The thought was absurdly ordinary after what had occurred, and perhaps it was that absurdity which caused her to laugh.

At first the sound was scarcely audible. Then it grew into a breathless, uneven amusement which she could neither explain nor suppress.

The carriage door opened.

The guard looked down at her with an expression of profound distress.

“Your Highness, are you injured?”

Talia attempted to answer, but another laugh escaped instead.

He stared at her helplessly.

Perhaps he believed her mad.

She could hardly blame him.

What sensible woman, having been publicly disgraced, rejected by the man she loved, and threatened by her own brother, would lie upon the floor of a carriage laughing over two broken fingernails?

Yet the more she considered the matter, the less she could restrain herself.

The guard remained at the door, uncertain what assistance he ought to offer, while Talia continued laughing until exhaustion finally overcame her.

In the days that followed, the Imperial Palace became a place of ceaseless activity.

The approaching pilgrimage of the Crown Prince and Princess Aila required preparations of unusual magnitude, for the journey was not merely a religious observance but an occasion of considerable political importance.

According to an ancient custom established in the reign of Emperor Darian, whose conquests had brought the ten kingdoms beneath a single crown, every descendant of the imperial line was expected to undertake a sacred journey upon reaching adulthood.

The custom had endured through generations of rulers, acquiring an importance which few dared question.

For princesses, the pilgrimage traditionally preceded marriage. Princes generally completed the journey after attaining their twentieth year.

Gares, however, had insisted that he and Aila should fulfil the obligation together.

They had entered the world upon the same day, he argued, and there could be no more fitting occasion for them to seek the blessings of heaven than a pilgrimage undertaken side by side.

The imperial household had accepted his reasoning.

Consequently, arrangements were made for an escort worthy of two individuals whose positions stood immediately beneath those of the Emperor and Empress.

Selected companies of the Imperial Knights were assigned to the expedition. Horses were inspected, provisions assembled, travelling carriages prepared, and an endless succession of officers summoned to attend upon the smallest details.

The command of the expedition had been entrusted to Varkas.

For Talia, this appointment proved both a blessing and a torment.

From the windows of the Separate Palace, she could observe the movements of the officers within the inner courtyard. It was there that Varkas frequently appeared, directing preparations with the same quiet authority that distinguished all his actions.

She soon discovered that she had developed an extraordinary interest in military arrangements.

The condition of the horses, the distribution of equipment, and the departure of supply wagons became matters of sudden importance, provided that Varkas happened to be present.

On one particular afternoon, a fine rain descended upon the palace grounds.

The courtyard stones glistened beneath the grey sky, and the distant roofs of the imperial buildings appeared softened by a veil of silver.

Talia stood beside an open window, resting her arms upon the sill.

Below, Varkas was inspecting a collection of travelling equipment. He spoke briefly with an officer, examined a saddle, and then turned his attention towards the horses waiting beneath the shelter of the stables.

The rain had dampened his dark hair.

He appeared entirely indifferent to the weather.

Talia watched him with the patient devotion of one who had long ago abandoned the pretence of indifference.

Every movement was familiar to her, and yet she never tired of observing him.

When he raised his head towards the overcast heavens, she found herself holding her breath.

The pale light touched his face, and the fine rain fell between them like a delicate curtain.

Something stirred within her memory.

She had seen him beneath such a sky before.

Long before the bitterness of the banquet, before the cruelty of their latest encounter, and before she had learned how deeply affection could wound the heart.

There had been another afternoon of rain.

Another moment when she had watched him without understanding that her life was about to change.

Talia lowered her eyes.

It was raining then, too.

And, standing at the window of the Separate Palace, she allowed herself to remember the day upon which she had first fallen in love with Varkas.
