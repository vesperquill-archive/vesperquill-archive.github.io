Chapter 10

In the aftermath of the dreadful commotion which had disturbed the peace of the Imperial Palace, Talia found herself confined almost entirely to the Separate Palace. The freedom she had once enjoyed in wandering through the gardens and corridors was abruptly withdrawn, and the little princess passed her days within chambers whose magnificence had long since ceased to afford her any pleasure. No one thought it necessary to explain the circumstances of her confinement, nor did anyone appear particularly concerned with the distress which the recent events had caused her. Nevertheless, the truth could not remain concealed for very long, for the maids, who ordinarily maintained a most uncommunicative silence whenever their young mistress addressed them, proved remarkably talkative when they believed themselves beyond her hearing.

Through their whispered conversations, Talia gradually learned that the boy who had assaulted her was none other than the Crown Prince of the Empire, the Emperor's legitimate son and her own elder half-brother. The little girl with dark hair and frightened green eyes, whom she had encountered beside the fountain in the secluded garden, was likewise a daughter of the Emperor and therefore her half-sister. Yet these discoveries, astonishing as they were, constituted only a small portion of the unhappy history which the servants so freely discussed. Their mother, the former Empress Bernadette, had died less than six months earlier, and Senevia had entered the Imperial Palace with her daughter scarcely three weeks after the deceased Empress had been laid to rest.

Talia had possessed only the vaguest understanding of the Emperor's former marriage. During her childhood at the Taren estate, the affairs of the imperial household had seemed remote and uninteresting, and she had never considered what consequences might follow her mother's elevation to the throne. Now, however, she began to comprehend that Senevia's arrival had brought considerable suffering to those who remained devoted to Bernadette's memory. Almost immediately upon assuming her new position, Senevia had ordered the removal of everything associated with her predecessor. Portraits had disappeared from the walls, familiar furnishings had been taken away, and the chambers once occupied by the late Empress had been transformed according to the tastes of their new mistress. Even the gardens had not escaped this determined alteration, for flowers were uprooted, trees replaced, and arrangements which had once been cherished were destroyed without hesitation.

It occurred to Talia that the beautiful little garden behind the Main Palace might have been one of the few places which had escaped her mother's attention. She remembered the white marble pavilion, the delicate fountain, and the beds of lavender and marigolds surrounding the bench upon which her half-sister had been seated. Unlike the splendid gardens adjoining the Separate Palace, that secluded retreat had possessed an atmosphere of intimacy, as though it had been arranged for the happiness of a particular family rather than for the admiration of visitors. Perhaps Bernadette had once spent peaceful afternoons there with her children. Perhaps the little princess had returned to the garden because it reminded her of the mother she had lost, and perhaps Talia's unexpected appearance had seemed to threaten even that last remaining refuge.

Such thoughts troubled her greatly, although she could not determine whether they were founded upon truth or merely upon her own unhappy imagination. She turned towards the window, beyond which the summer rain was falling heavily upon the garden that Senevia had ordered to be so carefully decorated. The luxuriant plants bowed beneath the weight of the water, while the dense foliage stirred in the wind and released the heavy scent of wet earth and grass. Once, Talia might have found the scene beautiful, but now the dark leaves and dripping branches seemed almost monstrous to her, as though the garden itself had become an unfriendly creature watching from beyond the glass.

Unable to endure the sight, she drew the curtains together and returned to her bed. Curling beneath the coverlet, she attempted to dismiss the memories which had tormented her since that dreadful afternoon, but the darkness only rendered them more vivid. She remembered the Crown Prince's furious green eyes, the terror upon her half-sister's face, and the cold expression of the fair-haired boy who had held the trembling girl protectively against him. Above all, she remembered the terrible difference between the warmth of his embrace beneath the spring rain and the hostility with which he had regarded her beside the fountain.

“Varkas Raedgo Sierkhan…”

She whispered the name into the silence of her chamber, scarcely recognising the sadness in her own voice. For weeks she had longed to discover what he was called, imagining that the knowledge would somehow bring her closer to him. Yet now that she knew his name, it afforded her not the slightest happiness. She could repeat it as often as she pleased, but she could not compel him to look upon her with kindness, nor could she recover the brief warmth which had made their first encounter so precious.

The servants' conversations had revealed something of his circumstances as well. Bernadette had belonged to the illustrious House of Oristein, one of the most ancient and distinguished families in Osiria, but through her mother's lineage she was also connected to the powerful House of Sierkhan. Thus Varkas, though only a distant relation, had shared a bond of kinship with the deceased Empress. Moreover, when he had first entered the Imperial Palace as a young boy to undertake the demanding education expected of one destined for military distinction, Bernadette had received him with considerable affection and watched over his welfare with the care of a devoted relative.

Talia could scarcely wonder, therefore, that he regarded Senevia with hostility. Her mother had entered the palace almost before the mourning for Bernadette had ended, assumed the dead woman's position, and ordered the removal of the possessions which had once testified to her existence. To those who had loved the former Empress, Senevia must have appeared less like a new sovereign than the living embodiment of an unforgivable injury.

And I am her daughter.

The thought settled upon Talia with a weight she could scarcely bear. She remembered the cold eyes which had looked upon her in the garden, and for the first time in her life, she felt something resembling resentment towards the very resemblance to Senevia of which she had once been so proud. Her deep blue eyes, her delicate features, and the beautiful face which had so often drawn comparisons to her mother now seemed to mark her unmistakably as the child of a woman whom others despised. It was as though she had inherited not merely Senevia's appearance, but also the hatred directed towards her.

Yet Talia could not bring herself to believe that this made her deserving of such cruelty. She had known nothing of Bernadette's suffering, had played no part in the Emperor's decisions, and had never intended to cause sorrow to either of her half-siblings. It was the Crown Prince who had seized her by the hair and beaten her while she lay helpless upon the grass. Whatever grief he had suffered, surely it could not make his actions just.

I was the one who was hurt. Why must I be the one who feels ashamed?

She repeated the question to herself with growing desperation, pressing her hands against the coverlet as though she might somehow hold back the bitterness rising within her.

I knew nothing. I did not hurt anyone. I have done nothing wicked.

These assurances became a kind of private refuge, words which she repeated whenever the memories threatened to overwhelm her. Yet each time she emerged from her chamber and encountered the cold, accusing faces of the servants, her conviction began to falter. Their hostility seemed so universal, so firmly established, that she sometimes wondered whether there must be some truth in the guilt they attributed to her.

The punishments inflicted upon her were seldom announced as punishments. They were administered beneath the respectable appearance of ordinary service, with a calculated carelessness that permitted the perpetrators to deny any deliberate cruelty. The water prepared for her bath was frequently so cold that she shivered violently upon entering it, while the maids scrubbed her skin with such unnecessary force that angry red marks remained long afterwards. When dressing her, their fingers sometimes moved with suspicious clumsiness, allowing sharp pins to pierce her skin. Her hair was combed so roughly that her scalp ached, and the meals brought to her table were often cold, stale, or scarcely fit to be served.

Talia understood perfectly well that these indignities were intentional, although she had long since ceased expecting kindness from the household. She was not entirely unfamiliar with such treatment, for during her years at the Taren estate, she had encountered servants who regarded her with disapproval and had grown accustomed to the small humiliations which accompanied her uncertain position within the family. At that time, however, she had possessed a defence against their contempt, something far more comforting than the magnificent title which had since been bestowed upon her.

She had Senevia.

Whenever Talia returned to her mother in tears, Senevia would draw her into a close embrace and speak with a tenderness which seemed capable of dispelling every sorrow. She would assure her daughter that she had been born of genuine love, and that the opinions of those who condemned them were unworthy of consideration. Talia had believed those words with all the unquestioning faith of childhood. She had learned to lift her chin, to meet hostile glances without lowering her eyes, and to behave as though the judgments of others could not wound her. So long as Senevia remained beside her, the world might whisper whatever it pleased.

But Senevia was no longer beside her. The woman who had once been her refuge now belonged to the Emperor and the imperial court, her days consumed by ceremonies, obligations, and affairs which left little room for the lonely child waiting in the Separate Palace. In her absence, the servants' whispers became more difficult to endure, particularly when they spoke of Bernadette with a devotion which seemed to transform the deceased Empress into the very embodiment of virtue.

They recalled her gentleness, her generosity, and the kindness with which she had treated even the humblest members of the household. They lamented the suffering she had endured and spoke of her death with a sorrow which had evidently lost none of its bitterness. Whenever Senevia's name entered their conversations, however, their voices altered, and the warmth of remembrance gave way to contempt. Talia heard enough to understand that the former Empress had been deeply loved, and that her own existence was regarded as part of the unhappiness which had destroyed that woman's peace.

The knowledge gradually changed her. The proud little girl who had once walked through the palace corridors with her head held high began to shrink beneath the attention of those around her. Her shoulders curved inward, her steps grew hesitant, and her eyes, formerly so quick to meet another person's gaze, became accustomed to studying the floor. She no longer protested as readily when the maids handled her roughly, nor did she demand explanations when her meals arrived cold. It seemed easier to endure their contempt in silence than to invite further hostility by attempting to defend herself.

The servants noticed this transformation with remarkable swiftness, and their cruelty increased accordingly. Since the Emperor paid so little attention to his daughter, and Senevia appeared scarcely more interested in the details of her daily life, they had little reason to fear that their conduct would ever be examined. To them, Talia was an imperial princess in name alone. They did not see a child who had been brought into the palace without understanding the circumstances of her birth; they saw the daughter of the woman who had displaced their beloved mistress, the living reminder of a relationship which they believed had brought humiliation and misery upon the late Empress.

Sometimes, as she passed through the corridors, Talia could hear their voices following her. The words were seldom spoken loudly enough to constitute an open insult, but their meaning was unmistakable. They discussed her parentage, her resemblance to Senevia, and the suffering of Bernadette with a deliberate carelessness that made it impossible for her not to hear. At such moments, she felt a fierce resentment rise within her and longed to turn upon them, demanding why they blamed her for matters over which she had never possessed the slightest control.

Yet whenever she considered defending herself, the memory of the Crown Prince's hatred returned. Perhaps, she thought miserably, there were people who had suffered so greatly because of her existence that she ought to endure a little unhappiness in return. It was a cruel conclusion for a child to reach, but loneliness had left her with no one to contradict it. Thus she endured, and the servants, discovering that she would neither complain nor resist, grew bolder still.

Until one morning, their cruelty exceeded even the limits of what Talia had taught herself to bear.

By then, nearly two seasons had passed since her arrival at the Imperial Palace. She descended to the dining room at the customary hour, expecting another of the dreary breakfasts which had become so familiar to her. Yet scarcely had she entered when she became conscious that something was amiss. There were more servants present than usual, and the maids stood along the walls in an orderly arrangement, their hands folded before them and their faces composed with an attentiveness which Talia had not witnessed in many weeks.

Several attendants moved quietly about the table, while others remained near the entrance as though awaiting some important event. Their unusual number filled her with a vague apprehension, and she paused upon the threshold, glancing from one face to another in search of some indication of what was expected of her. But the servants merely bowed and invited her to take her place with a politeness so extraordinary that it was almost alarming.

Then she saw the breakfast.

Instead of the hard, stale bread which had so often been placed before her, a basket of freshly baked rolls stood upon the spotless linen, their golden crusts glistening faintly beside a dish of rich butter. A roasted quail had been arranged upon a silver platter, while a deep bowl of steaming stew released a delicious fragrance into the room. The table had been laid with unusual care, and every dish appeared to have been prepared with the attention ordinarily reserved for an honoured guest.

Talia stopped beside her chair, scarcely able to believe what she was seeing. She had grown so accustomed to cold and unappetising meals that the sight of freshly prepared food seemed almost extravagant. The stew was thick with vegetables and other ingredients, its surface gleaming with the richness of butter and cream. It was nothing like the thin, flavourless soup she had been forced to swallow on so many previous mornings.

A painful emotion rose unexpectedly within her chest. She had once taken such meals for granted, and at the Taren estate she had complained if her breakfast was not served precisely as she preferred it. Now the mere sight of warm bread and fragrant stew brought her perilously close to tears. She glanced around the room, suddenly conscious of the many eyes directed towards her, but the servants maintained their respectful expressions, and the kitchen attendant who had presented the stew withdrew with an obedient bow.

For a moment, Talia allowed herself to hope.

Perhaps they have decided to stop. Perhaps they no longer hate me quite so much.

The possibility seemed almost too wonderful to contemplate, yet she could not prevent herself from imagining that the servants had at last grown weary of their cruelty. Perhaps they had decided that she had suffered enough. Perhaps, after so many months of coldness and humiliation, they were willing to regard her with something resembling kindness.

She seated herself carefully and took up her spoon, still scarcely daring to believe that she might enjoy a meal without fear. The stew was steaming, and when she dipped her spoon into the rich broth and raised it to her lips, the first mouthful proved so delicious that she almost forgot the uneasiness with which she had entered the room. Warmth spread across her tongue, followed by the gentle sweetness of vegetables and the rich flavours of milk and butter. The seasoning was delicate, and the ingredients had been cooked until tender.

For an instant, she simply sat there, overwhelmed by the forgotten pleasure of eating something warm and nourishing. Then hunger asserted itself with sudden force. She had endured so many unsatisfactory meals that the taste seemed to awaken an appetite she had scarcely realised she possessed. Forgetting the measured manners which her tutors had laboured to teach her, she began eating quickly, taking one spoonful after another with an eagerness she would ordinarily have considered quite improper.

The room remained quiet, but Talia scarcely noticed. Her attention was fixed upon the bowl before her, and for several precious moments, she allowed herself to believe that the morning might mark the beginning of something better. She imagined that perhaps the servants would continue treating her with courtesy, that the cold baths and painful dressing sessions might finally cease, and that she would no longer have to approach every meal with dread.

Then she tasted something strange.

At first, the unpleasant flavour was so faint that she thought she must have imagined it. She paused, holding the spoon above the bowl, and tried to distinguish the peculiar sensation lingering upon her tongue. It was not the ordinary richness of meat, nor the bitterness of an unfamiliar herb. There was something foul beneath the sweetness of the stew, an odour so disagreeable that her appetite diminished at once.

Talia frowned and lowered her spoon, examining the contents of the bowl with growing suspicion. The surface appeared perfectly ordinary, with pieces of vegetables floating in the thick broth, and nothing immediately suggested that the meal had been prepared improperly. She was about to dismiss her uneasiness when a faint sound reached her from behind.

Someone was laughing.

It was scarcely more than a suppressed breath, but Talia heard it distinctly. She turned towards the servants, and although the maids stood with their eyes lowered and their expressions carefully arranged into masks of indifference, she noticed a slight movement at the corners of several mouths. One young servant appeared to be struggling particularly hard to conceal a smile.

A cold sensation travelled down Talia's spine.

She looked back at the bowl. The servants had been watching her from the moment she entered, and the elaborate breakfast, their extraordinary politeness, and the unusual number of attendants suddenly assumed a meaning far more sinister than she had first imagined. Her hand began to tremble, and for several moments she sat motionless, unwilling to discover what she had begun to fear.

Then, very slowly, she lowered her spoon into the stew and began moving aside the larger pieces of food. She disturbed the thick broth cautiously, examining the bottom of the bowl until something heavy became visible beneath the vegetables. At first, she thought it was a portion of meat, for the object was dark and shapeless beneath the liquid, its outline obscured by the rich sauce. With a growing sense of dread, she slipped the spoon beneath it and lifted it towards the surface.

Then she saw what it was.

A dead rat lay within the stew, its grey body swollen by the thick broth and its small mouth hanging open. Its wet fur clung to its lifeless form, and the sight was so dreadful that for one terrible instant Talia could neither move nor breathe. Her mind refused to comprehend what her eyes had discovered, and she stared at the creature while the memory of every mouthful she had swallowed returned with sickening clarity.

She had eaten from the bowl. She had swallowed the broth in which the dead creature had been lying, had delighted in its warmth, and had believed, for a few precious moments, that the servants had finally decided to show her kindness.

The spoon slipped from her fingers.

Talia sprang from her chair so violently that it toppled behind her, but before she could take more than a step, her stomach convulsed. She bent forward and vomited upon the polished floor, the first painful spasm followed almost immediately by another. Her body shook with the violence of her retching, and although she struggled to draw breath, the sickness continued until she felt as though she had brought up everything she had eaten.

Tears streamed down her cheeks, blurring the room before her. The foul odour seemed to cling to the inside of her nose, growing stronger with every desperate breath, while the taste of the dead creature appeared to remain upon her tongue no matter how violently she gagged. Even when her stomach was empty, the retching would not cease, and she fell to her knees, one hand pressed against her aching abdomen while the other struggled to support her trembling body.

In her panic, she thrust her fingers into her mouth, desperately attempting to rid herself of the taste which seemed to have become permanently lodged there. She scraped at her tongue until her throat burned, struggling to force from her body anything that might remain of the dreadful meal. Yet there was nothing left for her to bring up, and the effort only left her weaker and more breathless.

At last, she collapsed forward upon the floor, trembling uncontrollably. Her vision was so blurred by tears that she could scarcely distinguish the furniture around her, and for several moments she could do nothing but struggle for breath while the dreadful sensation continued to overwhelm her.

Then she heard footsteps.

Talia raised her head with difficulty, expecting someone to approach her. Surely, whatever resentment the servants bore towards her, they could not simply stand and watch a child suffer in such a manner. Surely one of them would fetch water, summon a physician, or at least offer a cloth with which she might wipe her face.

But no one came.

Through her tear-blurred vision, she saw the kitchen maid calmly removing the dishes from the table. Another servant gathered the silverware, while a third began wiping the polished surface with the same meticulous care she might have employed after an ordinary breakfast. Their movements were unhurried and perfectly composed, and they passed around the trembling child without offering so much as a glance in her direction.

The fallen chair was set upright, the plates were carried away, and the tablecloth was straightened. Every trace of the meal was removed with quiet efficiency, while Talia remained upon the floor beside the remains of her breakfast, too exhausted even to protest.

It was as though she had ceased to exist.

Only then did she understand the full cruelty of what had been done. The warm bread, the fragrant stew, the respectful bows, and the elaborate preparations had never been gestures of reconciliation. They had been part of the punishment itself, carefully arranged to awaken her hope before destroying it. The servants had wanted her to believe that they had finally accepted her, wanted her to enjoy the food with innocent gratitude, and had waited for the moment when she would discover what they had concealed beneath the rich broth.

And now, having accomplished their purpose, they were returning to their ordinary duties as though nothing of consequence had occurred.

Talia watched them in silence, her small body trembling against the cold floor. The servants continued moving about the dining room, clearing away every trace of the breakfast with an indifference more terrible than any insult they might have spoken. No one asked whether she was ill, no one offered assistance, and no one acknowledged the tears which continued to fall down her cheeks.

Surrounded by the quiet sounds of silverware being gathered and dishes being carried away, the little princess remained where she had fallen, unable to understand how so many people could witness her suffering and behave as though she were not there.
