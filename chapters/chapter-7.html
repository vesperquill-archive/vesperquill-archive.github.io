Chapter 7

The boy's outstretched hand remained suspended between them, pale against the dark folds of his cloak. Talia regarded it with an interest she could scarcely explain. His fingers were long and finely shaped, possessing an elegance she had seldom observed even among the gentlemen who attended her mother's gatherings. Indeed, his hands appeared almost as fair as her own, though their appearance suggested a strength quite beyond anything she might have expected from one so young.

For an instant, she forgot her predicament and began to reach towards him. Then a faint movement against her palms reminded her of the small creature she had undertaken to rescue, and she drew back with sudden alarm.

“I cannot give you my hand,” she declared. “I have something to look after.”

The boy's expression altered very slightly. Beneath the hood, which had grown heavy with rain, his pale eyes travelled towards her clasped fingers.

“What have you there?”

Talia hesitated. She had no desire to reveal the miserable appearance of the little bird, particularly to a stranger whose immaculate bearing made her own condition seem doubly disgraceful.

“Something I found.”

“Is it worth all this trouble?”

She considered the question with an earnestness which might have amused anyone less solemn than her companion.

“I cannot say that it is worth very much.”

“Then why burden yourself with it?”

“Because,” she replied impatiently, “a thing need not be valuable to deserve being saved.”

The boy regarded her in silence. A slight crease appeared between his brows, and Talia began to fear that she had exhausted whatever small measure of patience had persuaded him to descend into the pit.

She imagined him turning away, leaving her to contend with the mud and rain as best she could. The prospect was so dreadful that she nearly offered an apology.

Before she could speak, however, he bent towards her.

Talia had scarcely time to understand his intention before he lifted her from the ground.

A startled cry escaped her.

“What are you doing?”

“Getting you out.”

“But—”

“Do not move about so much.”

The instruction was delivered without the slightest alteration in his voice, yet it possessed such unquestionable authority that Talia obeyed at once.

She found herself held securely against him, one of his arms supporting her while the other maintained his balance upon the treacherous ground. Her first impulse was to protest against being handled with so little ceremony, but the sensation of being lifted effortlessly from the mud left her too astonished to complain.

Instead, she occupied herself with protecting the bird.

She loosened her fingers just enough to assure herself that the little creature could breathe, then drew her hands closer to her chest as the boy began his ascent.

He moved with remarkable certainty. Where Talia had slipped and stumbled, he found secure places for his feet, advancing up the muddy slope with the quiet agility of a creature accustomed to difficult ground. The rain had made the earth almost impossibly treacherous, yet he neither faltered nor showed any sign of regretting his undertaking.

Within a short while, they reached the garden above.

Talia breathed a sigh of relief.

Her rescuer, however, appeared considerably less satisfied.

He glanced down at his clothing. The lower portion of his cloak had been stained with mud, his fine trousers were scarcely recognisable beneath the splashes, and his boots had suffered so severely that Talia doubted even the most diligent servant could restore them without complaint.

A shadow of displeasure crossed his face.

“This is a thoroughly disagreeable business,” he observed.

Talia felt an immediate pang of guilt.

Although she had never been particularly inclined to consider the inconvenience she caused others, she could hardly deny that the boy had sacrificed the cleanliness of his attire for her benefit.

“I shall see that you are compensated,” she announced.

He looked at her.

“My mother has many servants, and she is exceedingly wealthy. I can have someone bring you a new cloak and boots. Better ones, if you wish.”

The boy said nothing.

Encouraged by his silence, Talia continued.

“And I shall make certain that you receive a reward. You need only tell me your name.”

His expression grew colder.

“Do you always speak as though you are granting favours to your subjects?”

Talia stared at him.

“I was merely attempting to be generous!”

“You have an unusual manner of showing gratitude.”

Her cheeks burned.

Ordinarily, she would never have tolerated such insolence. She had only recently entered the Imperial Palace, but she had already been instructed often enough in the importance of her rank. No boy, however handsomely dressed, ought to address the Emperor's daughter in such a manner.

Yet she discovered, to her considerable annoyance, that she could think of nothing sufficiently clever to say.

It was not fear that silenced her.

At least, she did not believe it was fear.

There was simply something about the boy's manner that unsettled her. He neither flattered nor appeared intimidated by her, and his indifference made her painfully conscious of how ridiculous she must look in her ruined green gown.

The rain continued to fall, cold against her forehead and cheeks, but her face remained inexplicably warm.

The boy carried her a little farther, towards the shelter of a great tree which had somehow escaped the destruction of the surrounding garden. Its broad branches offered some protection from the rain, though the leaves above them were already heavy with water.

He had begun to lower her when a tiny sound interrupted him.

The bird stirred.

The boy paused.

For the first time since their encounter, his attention seemed wholly captured by the small bundle sheltered between Talia's hands.

“What is that?”

Talia looked down.

“A bird,” she answered, somewhat reluctantly.

He regarded the creature with evident surprise.

“Are you certain?”

Talia's indignation returned at once.

“Of course I am certain!”

She opened her hands a little wider, though the sight thus revealed was hardly likely to strengthen her argument.

The poor fledgling was in a lamentable condition. Its feathers had been flattened by the rain, its wings were coated with mud, and its thin little body looked scarcely capable of supporting itself. In truth, it bore little resemblance to the cheerful birds Talia had seen hopping among the hedges at the Taren estate.

She felt a sudden, unreasonable embarrassment.

“It was not always quite so dreadful-looking,” she said.

The boy glanced at her.

“Was it not?”

“Well, perhaps it was never especially beautiful,” she admitted. “But that is hardly its fault.”

He made no reply.

Talia wondered whether he was laughing at her inwardly. His face revealed nothing, and she found herself wishing that he would smile, if only so she might discover whether he was capable of doing so.

Instead, he adjusted his hold upon her and drew her clasped hands beneath the shelter of his hood.

The movement brought her unexpectedly close to him.

Talia became conscious of the warmth beneath his cloak, and of the extraordinary contrast between his skin and her own chilled fingers.

He was warm.

Not merely less cold than she was, but possessed of a comforting warmth which seemed almost impossible beneath the miserable weather.

The little bird evidently reached the same conclusion.

It stirred against her palms, then pressed its tiny body towards the shelter offered by the boy's neck.

He lowered his gaze.

“Your hands are freezing,” he remarked. “Have you been outside all afternoon?”

“I do not know how long.”

“That much is evident.”

Talia ought to have resented the observation. Instead, she found herself examining his face.

They were so near one another that she could distinguish individual drops of rain upon his lashes. His features, which had appeared severe from a distance, possessed a delicacy she had not expected. Yet it was his eyes which held her attention.

She had never seen eyes of quite that colour.

They reminded her of the winter sky above the Taren estate, when the clouds parted after a snowfall and the pale sunlight illuminated the frozen fields. But within their blue depths there appeared to be something brighter, something which caught the light in tiny, scattered fragments.

Talia stared with the frank curiosity of a child.

“Your eyes are very strange,” she whispered.

The boy looked at her.

“Are they?”

“Yes. There are little silver crowns in them.”

For the first time, his composure faltered.

His eyes widened slightly, and his lips parted as though he had intended to reply. Whatever words had occurred to him, however, remained unspoken.

Talia waited.

He continued looking at her.

It was a curious experience. She had grown accustomed to being examined by strangers since entering the palace, but their attention was generally accompanied by judgment or calculation.

This boy's gaze was different.

He seemed to be searching for something he had not expected to find.

Talia wondered what he saw when he looked into her eyes.

Are mine strange as well?

She nearly asked him.

Then a distant voice interrupted the quiet beneath the tree.

“Miss Talia!”

Talia started.

She knew that voice.

Her nurse had evidently discovered her absence and was searching the grounds with the desperation of a woman who expected to be held personally responsible for every misfortune that might befall her charge.

“Miss Talia! Where have you gone?”

The familiar title made Talia wince.

Since their arrival at court, her mother had repeatedly insisted that the household address her daughter according to her imperial rank. Yet the nurse, who had known Talia long before she became a princess, frequently returned to the habits of earlier years.

Senevia regarded these lapses as intolerable.

Talia had never understood why a few words should matter so greatly.

Now, however, she was concerned with something else entirely.

She looked at the boy.

“I must go.”

The words sounded strangely disagreeable.

She had spent much of the afternoon wishing to escape the rain, and yet the prospect of leaving the shelter of his arms filled her with an unexpected reluctance.

The boy remained still.

For a moment, Talia wondered whether he had failed to hear her.

Then he lowered her carefully to the ground.

The warmth of his embrace disappeared, and the cold air seemed to rush into the space it had occupied. Talia shivered, suddenly aware of how thoroughly the rain had soaked her clothes.

She looked down at the bird.

It had grown a little quieter beneath the shelter of the boy's cloak, but its condition remained uncertain.

A thought occurred to her.

“Will you do something for me?”

He regarded her with his customary reserve.

“What is it?”

She held out the little creature.

“Would you look after him?”

The boy glanced from the bird to her face.

“Why?”

“Because you are much warmer than I am,” she answered.

It was the simplest explanation she could offer, and perhaps the truest.

The boy considered her for a moment before extending his hands.

Talia transferred the fledgling into his care with extraordinary caution. His fingers closed gently around it, and he drew the small creature towards the warmth beneath his hood.

The sight pleased her more than she could have imagined.

Despite his stern expression and disagreeable manner, he handled the bird with a tenderness which seemed entirely natural.

Perhaps, she thought, he was not nearly so cold-hearted as he wished others to believe.

“Do you think it will recover?” she asked.

He examined the little creature.

Talia watched his face anxiously.

She had no reason to suppose that he possessed any special knowledge of birds, but she found herself placing an unreasonable amount of faith in his answer.

At length, he looked at her.

“I believe it has a fair chance.”

“Truly?”

“If it is kept warm and cared for.”

Talia considered this.

“Then you must take very good care of it.”

“I shall do what I can.”

She smiled.

For some reason, she believed him completely.

The boy's expression remained composed, yet something about his eyes seemed different. Talia could not have explained the change, but she imagined that, had he permitted himself the smallest smile, it would have looked very much like the expression he wore at that moment.

The nurse called again, closer this time.

Talia glanced towards the palace.

“I really must go now.”

The boy inclined his head.

She hesitated.

There were several questions she wished to ask him. She did not know his name, nor why he had been walking alone through the deserted garden, nor whether he lived within the palace grounds.

Most of all, she wanted to know whether she might see him again.

Yet the questions remained unspoken.

Instead, she gathered the ruined skirts of her gown and began to run.

The garden was a dreadful confusion of mud, uprooted shrubs, and abandoned heaps of earth. Talia hurried between them, splashing through puddles without any further concern for her appearance.

After several steps, however, she stopped.

Something compelled her to look back.

The boy remained beneath the great tree.

His dark cloak stood out against the pale rain, and he appeared to be watching the path along which she had gone.

Talia's heart gave a curious little movement.

Why does he remain there?

Perhaps he was waiting for the rain to diminish.

Perhaps he had no particular destination.

Or perhaps he was watching her.

She found the last possibility strangely pleasing.

For a moment, she imagined returning to his side. They might wait together until the rain ceased, and perhaps someone would bring them warm blankets and something pleasant to eat.

She could picture a cheerful fire burning in a quiet room, the little bird resting safely nearby while she and the strange blue-eyed boy watched over it.

The thought filled her with a happiness so sudden that she almost turned back.

But before she could act upon the impulse, a familiar figure emerged from the palace entrance.

“There you are!”

Her nurse hurried towards her, her round face flushed with exertion and alarm.

“Do you know how long I have been searching for you?”

Talia opened her mouth, but the woman had already seized her hand.

“And what have you done to your clothes? Good heavens, child, you look as though you have been dragged through a stable!”

“I fell,” Talia answered.

“Fell? Where?”

“In the garden.”

“In this weather? Have you taken leave of your senses?”

The nurse looked her over with increasing horror.

“Your mother has been asking for you, and you are expected to be presented before His Majesty! Whatever shall we do with you in such a state?”

Talia allowed herself to be pulled towards the palace, too distracted to offer her customary objections.

She wanted to explain about the bird.

She wanted to tell her nurse that a boy had helped her, that he had carried her out of the mud, and that he possessed the most remarkable eyes she had ever seen.

But she suspected that the nurse would not be interested in any of these matters.

The woman was already speaking of baths, clean gowns, and the disgrace that would follow if Talia appeared before the Emperor looking as she did.

They reached the covered passage leading into the Separate Palace.

Talia looked back once more.

The great tree was still visible beyond the curtain of rain.

But the boy had disappeared.

She searched the garden with her eyes, hoping to discover some trace of his dark cloak among the mist and branches.

There was nothing.

Only the rain, the deserted paths, and the muddy hollow from which he had rescued her.

Talia felt a disappointment she could neither explain nor dismiss.

She had not even learned his name.

And yet, as her nurse hurried her along the corridor, she found that she could remember every detail of his face.

Most clearly of all, she remembered his eyes.

Those strange, beautiful blue eyes, in which she had imagined seeing little silver crowns.
