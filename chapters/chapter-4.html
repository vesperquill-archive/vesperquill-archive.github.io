Chapter 4

There were gentlemen whose arrival at court was announced with all the ceremony their vanity could command, and there were others whose distinction rendered such ceremonies entirely unnecessary. Varkas Raedgo Sierkhan belonged unmistakably to the latter.

When the attendant at the grand entrance prepared to proclaim his name, Varkas dismissed the intended announcement with a slight movement of his gloved hand. He had never possessed any fondness for the theatrical customs of the nobility, and the prospect of becoming the object of a hundred curious observations appeared to afford him little pleasure.

Unfortunately, the court was not inclined to respect his preference. As he descended the broad marble staircase, a curious stillness spread through the assembly. Conversations diminished into whispers, ladies turned their heads, and gentlemen interrupted their discussions. Even those who had been attending upon the Crown Prince found themselves momentarily distracted.

Varkas seemed unconscious of the disturbance. He walked with that unstudied dignity which Talia had always found more captivating than the most elaborate demonstrations of courtly elegance. From her position near the centre of the ballroom, she watched him with an attention bordering upon desperation.

There he is.

How many evenings had she spent recalling the precise manner in which he carried himself? How often had she imagined his face when she ought to have been occupied with matters of considerably greater importance? And yet, confronted with the reality of his presence, she discovered that memory had been a poor substitute.

He wore the dark ceremonial uniform appropriate to his rank, though the insignia displayed upon his breast distinguished him from the knights whose duties ordinarily placed him within the imperial household. Tonight, he appeared not merely as an officer in service to the throne, but as the acknowledged successor to one of the East's most distinguished noble houses.

The difference was subtle, but its meaning was unmistakable. He had come as a future duke, and as the man who would soon marry Princess Aila.

Talia felt her fingers grow cold around the stem of her goblet.

Varkas approached the royal gathering without hesitation. The Crown Prince received him with an expression of familiar satisfaction, while Aila's countenance brightened with an affection she made no attempt to conceal.

“Your Imperial Highness,” Varkas said, bowing.

“Varkas,” Gares replied. “You have arrived at last.”

“I trust I have not kept the company waiting.”

“Only those who have been looking forward to seeing you.”

Gares's glance moved towards his sister. Aila lowered her eyes, though not before Talia had witnessed the delighted colour rising in her cheeks.

“How very considerate of you to come,” the princess said.

Varkas inclined his head. “I would not have neglected an occasion of such importance.”

There was nothing extraordinary in his reply. It was courteous, restrained, and perfectly suitable to the circumstances. Yet Talia felt each word with the force of a personal injury.

When Aila extended her hand, Varkas accepted it. The gesture lasted scarcely a moment, but to Talia, it seemed an eternity.

Must you touch her? Is it not enough that she possesses your name, your future, and the blessing of every person in this wretched palace? Must I witness even the smallest tenderness between you?

The question rose within her with such violence that she nearly spoke it aloud. Her grip tightened around the goblet, and the Count standing beside her observed the movement with growing concern.

“Your Highness,” he said quietly, “I wonder whether the air in the gardens might prove more agreeable.”

Talia did not turn. “Have you suddenly developed an interest in horticulture, Count?”

“Only in the preservation of delicate things.”

“How fortunate that I am not delicate.”

“I had begun to suspect as much.”

She glanced at him and discovered that, for once, his expression was not entirely amused.

“You would advise me to withdraw?”

“I would advise you to consider whether remaining will bring you any pleasure.”

Talia looked again towards the royal gathering. Aila was smiling, Varkas stood at her side, and Gares appeared altogether satisfied with the world. Around them, the nobility offered congratulations with the eager sincerity reserved for those whose happiness had been sanctioned by rank and fortune.

Leave? And allow them to enjoy their triumph without the slightest interruption?

The very suggestion restored something of her determination.

“You mistake me, Count. I have only just begun to enjoy myself.”

She placed her goblet upon a nearby table, adjusted the folds of her gown, and began to walk. The Count made no further attempt to restrain her. Indeed, as she passed, she thought she detected the return of his customary smile.

The company parted before her. Some moved aside from respect for her title, while others did so because they had recognised the expression upon her face and wished to observe whatever might follow from a safer distance. Talia cared little for their motives.

She approached the Crown Prince with a composure so carefully arranged that it was almost convincing.

“My dear brother,” she said pleasantly. “And my beloved sister. What an unexpected pleasure to find you both surrounded by so many admirers.”

Gares's expression hardened at once. Aila, however, smiled.

“Talia. How kind of you to join us.”

“How could I possibly remain absent from such a happy occasion?”

Her gaze rested upon Aila, and her smile acquired a sharper edge.

“I confess I had wondered whether you would be sufficiently recovered to attend another grand assembly. Your last appearance was rather memorable.”

Aila regarded her without visible discomfort.

“I am quite well, thank you.”

“Indeed? I am relieved.”

“You are most thoughtful.”

The reply was so gentle that several ladies standing nearby exchanged approving glances. Talia noticed their reaction immediately. Of course they admired her. Aila could transform even an insult into an opportunity to display her refinement.

Does nothing disturb you? Have you never known the desire to strike back?

For a moment, Talia wondered whether her sister possessed a strength of character she herself could never attain. Then she rejected the thought with furious contempt. It was easy to remain gracious when the world had never denied one anything.

Gares stepped forward.

“I had hoped,” he said coldly, “that you might possess sufficient judgment to stay away.”

“From a celebration held in my own father's palace?”

“Do not presume upon that relationship.”

Talia smiled.

“Would you prefer that I address him as His Imperial Majesty whenever you are present?”

“I would prefer that you conduct yourself with some understanding of your position.”

“And what position would satisfy you, brother? Behind a curtain? Beneath a staircase? Perhaps somewhere beyond the palace walls?”

“Anywhere that does not oblige decent company to endure your insolence.”

A murmur passed through the nearest spectators. Talia felt their attention gathering around her once more, and a strange satisfaction stirred within her. She had obtained an audience, and now she intended to keep it.

“How unfortunate,” she said, “that we should disagree so profoundly upon the meaning of decency.”

Gares's jaw tightened.

“You have already disgraced yourself sufficiently this evening.”

“Have I? I was under the impression that my gown had been much admired.”

“Your appearance is an affront to this household.”

Talia looked down at the elaborate fabric of her dress. Then, with deliberate slowness, she spread the skirts between her fingers.

“What a curious judgment. My mother has always spoken of this gown with extraordinary affection.”

Gares went still.

“She wore it upon an occasion of particular importance in her youth,” Talia continued. “I believe it was an evening upon which she made a most lasting impression upon His Majesty.”

The surrounding company fell silent. Several guests suddenly discovered urgent reasons to study the decorations, while others exchanged looks of scarcely concealed alarm.

Talia's smile deepened.

“Surely a garment associated with such a cherished memory cannot be altogether unsuitable.”

“You spiteful—”

“Brother,” Aila interrupted.

Her hand came to rest upon his arm. Gares turned towards her with an expression of scarcely controlled anger, but Aila merely gave the slightest shake of her head before looking once more at Talia.

“The gown is beautiful,” she said. “And you wear it very well.”

Talia stared at her.

It would have been easier had Aila answered with an insult. She might have endured anger, and she might even have welcomed it. But this unfailing gentleness made her feel as though every attempt to wound her sister merely revealed the ugliness of her own intentions.

Do you imagine yourself above me? Is that why you refuse even to be angry?

Aila turned towards Varkas, and her expression softened.

“There is something I have wished to share with you, Talia.”

“How fortunate. I was beginning to fear we had exhausted our conversation.”

“We have received confirmation of our wedding arrangements.”

The words were spoken with such quiet happiness that Talia felt the ballroom shift beneath her feet. Aila continued, unaware—or unwilling to acknowledge—the effect she had produced.

“The journey eastward has been arranged, and the necessary ceremonies are being prepared. There is much to accomplish before the season changes.”

“How industrious everyone must be.”

“It has all happened rather quickly.”

“Has it?”

“Yes. I scarcely know whether to feel delighted or frightened.”

She laughed softly, and Varkas looked down at her. Talia could not bear to interpret his expression.

“Surely you are not frightened of marriage,” she said.

“Not of marriage itself. Only of leaving behind everything familiar.”

“How touching.”

Aila did not appear to notice the sarcasm.

“I have lived within these walls for so long that the thought of another home seems almost impossible. Yet I know I shall be happy.”

She rested her hand upon Varkas's sleeve, and Talia's attention fixed upon it. That small, graceful hand seemed to possess an ease and confidence which Talia had never known. It claimed its place without hesitation, without fear, and without the slightest expectation of being refused.

Take it away. Take your hand away from him.

Her heart beat painfully, but Aila continued speaking with the same serene happiness.

“There will be religious observances before our departure, of course, and I expect the coming weeks will be terribly occupied. We may have few opportunities to meet again.”

“Then this is a farewell?”

“Perhaps not entirely. But I wished to speak to you before everything became so hurried.”

“How considerate.”

“I hope that whatever disagreements have existed between us, you may remember this evening kindly.”

Talia looked at her in disbelief.

“Kindly?”

Aila's smile remained unchanged.

“Yes. I should like us to part without bitterness.”

The words struck Talia as the most exquisite cruelty. Without bitterness—as though bitterness were a trivial inconvenience, something to be laid aside as easily as an unfashionable ribbon. As though she could simply decide to cease wanting what had never belonged to her.

She recalled the Count's earlier warning and, for one brief moment, wondered whether she ought to have followed his advice. Yet she had already come too far to retreat.

Aila turned slightly towards the guests.

“I hope everyone will enjoy the remainder of the celebration.”

The conversation, it seemed, was concluded. Talia watched her sister prepare to depart, and a sudden determination seized her.

“Wait.”

Aila paused.

“Yes?”

“You have offered me so many gracious sentiments that I should be ashamed to let you go without expressing one of my own.”

A faint uncertainty appeared upon the princess's face.

“Talia—”

“Surely you will permit me to wish you happiness?”

The surrounding guests grew attentive. Talia reached for the goblet she had taken up again during their exchange and raised it slightly.

“To your future, sister.”

Aila hesitated.

Varkas moved.

It was scarcely more than a shift of his weight, but Talia noticed it instantly. His attention, which had appeared so remote throughout the conversation, was now fixed entirely upon her. He understood. Perhaps he had understood from the moment she approached.

And when he placed himself a little nearer to Aila, something within Talia finally gave way.

Even now. Even before I have done anything, you stand ready to defend her.

She laughed, though it was a strange, breathless sound that bore little resemblance to amusement.

“How fortunate you are, Aila.”

The princess frowned.

“I beg your pardon?”

“To possess so many devoted guardians.”

“Talia, I do not understand.”

“No,” she whispered. “I do not suppose you ever could.”

Her hand moved.

The wine swept from the goblet in a dark arc. For one suspended instant, the liquid seemed to hang between them, brilliant beneath the chandeliers. Then it struck Aila's white gown.

A cry rose from the company.

The stain spread across the delicate fabric, deepening as it travelled downward through the embroidered folds. Against the pale silk, its colour appeared dreadful, resembling the terrible evidence of a wound rather than the mere ruin of an expensive garment.

Aila stood motionless, looking down at herself with her lips parted in astonishment. Talia watched with a satisfaction so fierce that it almost frightened her.

There was something grotesquely beautiful about the ruined gown. Only moments earlier, Aila had seemed the very image of an immaculate bride. Now the splendour of her attire had been transformed into a spectacle of humiliation.

“How unfortunate,” Talia murmured.

No one answered.

She lifted her eyes to her sister's face.

“Perhaps you will remember this evening after all.”

Aila's expression changed. For the first time, the serene composure that had so infuriated Talia began to fail.

“Talia,” she whispered.

There was genuine fear in her voice, and Talia, who had longed for precisely such a moment, discovered that its arrival brought her a satisfaction almost painful in its intensity.

“You spoke of blessings,” she said softly. “I wonder whether the heavens are always so generous to those who expect their favour.”

Aila recoiled.

“Talia, please.”

“May your happiness prove less secure than you imagine.”

The silence that followed was dreadful. Somewhere nearby, a lady gasped, and Gares uttered an oath, but Talia scarcely heard either.

She was watching Aila. At last, her sister no longer appeared untouchable. At last, the perfect princess looked frightened.

For one fleeting instant, Talia wanted to laugh.

Then a hand closed around her wrist.

The force of it startled her so violently that the empty goblet slipped from her fingers. She was drawn backward before she could recover her balance, and the ballroom, the watching nobility, and even Aila's ruined gown seemed to disappear from her awareness.

She knew whose hand held her. She knew before she looked.

Yet she had spent the entire evening avoiding his eyes, and now there was nowhere left to turn.

Slowly, unwillingly, she raised her face.

Varkas stood before her. The cold blue of his eyes seemed almost luminous beneath the chandeliers, and there was no tenderness in them, nor any trace of the distant courtesy with which he had greeted the Crown Prince.

There was only anger—a cold, controlled, and merciless fury that seemed all the more dreadful for its restraint.

Talia felt the last of her triumph collapse, and for the first time that evening, she was afraid.
