Chapter 11

From that dreadful day onward, Talia found herself unable to eat almost anything. The mere sight of a prepared meal filled her with uneasiness, for she could no longer trust what might be concealed beneath its surface. No matter how appetising the food appeared, the memory of what she had discovered in her breakfast remained painfully vivid.

Her nanny, unable to comprehend the sudden change in her appetite, regarded it as nothing more than childish obstinacy. She scolded Talia for being excessively particular about her meals, never once suspecting the true cause of her refusal. Talia, for her part, offered no explanation and sustained herself upon the occasional portions of fruit and honey brought to her as refreshments.

Loneliness and solitude had ceased to be her greatest misfortunes. Within the most magnificent and luxurious residence in the Empire, the young princess now found herself engaged in a miserable struggle against starvation.

There were occasions when hunger became so unbearable that she reluctantly attempted to eat the dishes prepared by the servants. Yet these efforts invariably ended in fresh horror. Sometimes she discovered insects concealed amongst the food; on other occasions, she encountered small mice or repulsive clumps of tightly curled hair whose origin she could not bear to contemplate.

After several such experiences, even the most desperate hunger could no longer persuade her to approach a proper meal. Within a few weeks, her cheeks had become hollow, her delicate wrists appeared painfully thin, and the once-bright eyes which had so closely resembled Senevia's seemed unnaturally large in her wasted face.

At length, even her inattentive nanny could no longer dismiss the alteration as a passing whim. Alarmed by the child's increasingly fragile condition, she hurried to the Empress and declared, with considerable agitation, that Her Majesty's only daughter appeared to be wasting away before their very eyes.

It was through this intervention that Talia finally saw her mother again, after several months of absence.

“Good heavens, Talia. Whatever has happened to you? How have you come to look so dreadfully unwell?”

Those were the first words Senevia spoke upon entering the Separate Palace. The woman who had seemingly forgotten her daughter's existence now stood before her, examining her diminished appearance with evident astonishment.

In painful contrast to Talia's own miserable condition, Senevia appeared as radiant as a flower in the height of summer. Her complexion was flawless, her elegant figure was adorned in splendid fabrics, and her beauty seemed almost untouched by the passage of time.

At the sight of that familiar face, Talia's eyes filled with tears. She had intended to be angry, to demand why her mother had abandoned her for so long, and to reproach her for the selfishness which had left her daughter utterly alone. Yet when she finally opened her mouth, every accusation dissolved into a broken sob.

She began to weep with the helpless abandon of a much younger child. Between her tears, she recounted everything that had happened within the Separate Palace, from the freezing baths and painful treatment of the maids to the dreadful cruelty inflicted upon her at mealtimes.

Nothing was omitted. She confessed every humiliation, every injury, and every miserable experience she had endured while waiting for someone to come to her assistance.

Throughout the entire account, Senevia remained seated beside the bed, listening without interruption. Her expression revealed little, but Talia interpreted her silence as evidence of an anger too profound to be expressed.

Surely her mother was struggling to contain her indignation. Surely she was so horrified by the treatment of her only daughter that she could scarcely determine how severely the offenders ought to be punished.

Encouraged by this belief, Talia seized Senevia's arm and shook it desperately.

“Mother, I beg you, do not permit them to torment me any longer! You must put an end to this at once. Please, promise me that none of them shall ever be allowed to hurt me again!”

Senevia regarded her for a moment before inclining her head slightly.

“And why, my dear, should I be obliged to do that?”

Talia stared at her in bewilderment. The question had been uttered without irritation or mockery, and it was precisely this absence of emotion which made it so incomprehensible.

Her mother's expression betrayed nothing beyond a mild and apparently sincere curiosity, as though she truly could not understand why a frightened child should appeal to her for protection.

“Talia, surely you understand that this palace belongs to you, and that every servant within its walls is placed at your disposal. You are nine years of age now, and no longer an infant. Must you truly come weeping to your mother merely because you have proved incapable of managing your own household?”

For several moments, Talia could find no words.

She had believed that Senevia would be horrified by the servants' conduct. Yet her mother spoke as though the disgrace lay not in their cruelty, but in Talia's inability to prevent it.

Senevia raised one graceful hand and cupped her daughter's cheek. Studying the thin little face before her, she released a quiet sigh of disappointment.

“You are a daughter of His Imperial Majesty. I confess I find it almost beyond comprehension that you should permit yourself to be so thoroughly humiliated by persons of no consequence. It is rather embarrassing to discover that my own daughter possesses so little spirit and such lamentable innocence.”

“M-Mother…”

The word escaped Talia scarcely above a whisper. She had no answer to offer, for the person before her seemed suddenly unfamiliar, despite possessing the same beautiful face which had once been the centre of her entire world.

Senevia turned her gaze towards the candle burning near the window, its small flame casting a wavering light across her exquisite features. There was no anger on Talia's behalf, no indignation at the injuries she had suffered, and no trace of the maternal protectiveness which the child had so desperately expected.

Instead, the Empress appeared merely disappointed, as though she were considering how best to instruct a particularly slow-witted pupil.

Watching her, Talia experienced a dreadful sensation of estrangement. The woman seated beside her was undeniably her mother, yet something in her manner seemed so devoid of ordinary human tenderness that Talia could scarcely reconcile her with the loving presence she remembered from childhood.

It was almost as though some strange and unfeeling creature had learned to imitate the appearance and behaviour of a beautiful woman.

After a considerable silence, Senevia appeared to arrive at a satisfactory conclusion. She snapped her fingers lightly, and her expression brightened.

“Ah, I believe I have thought of something. I shall place a capable guard at your disposal. He is a man whom I have taken considerable pains to discipline over the years, and I assure you that his obedience has been thoroughly established. If you possess sufficient sense to employ him properly, I daresay you will find him exceedingly useful.”

Having delivered this proposal, she rose from her chair and smoothed the folds of her gown, evidently regarding the matter as settled.

But Talia had not endured months of misery merely to be offered another stranger in place of the person whose presence she desired above all others.

She reached forward and caught the hem of her mother's dress with trembling fingers.

“I do not want a guard! I do not care how useful he may be! Mother, please, I only wish to remain with you. Let me stay by your side, as I did before!”

Her voice broke upon the final words, and she clung desperately to the costly fabric. For one brief instant, an expression of unmistakable revulsion passed across Senevia's beautiful face.

It vanished almost immediately, but Talia had seen it. The discovery left her so profoundly shocked that the colour drained from her cheeks.

Senevia lowered her eyes towards the small hands gripping her gown. With deliberate composure, she loosened Talia's fingers one by one, freeing the fabric without the slightest appearance of haste.

Then she bent towards her daughter and clicked her tongue softly, her expression assuming the indulgent pity one might bestow upon a child incapable of understanding the simplest lesson.

“My poor, foolish Talia. Have you truly understood nothing of what I have been telling you? All this unpleasantness began, in one fashion or another, because of me. Yet tell me, my dear, have you ever wondered why no one has thought to place a dead mouse in my soup?”

Talia remained perfectly still.

The question, spoken in so gentle a voice, seemed more frightening than an angry rebuke. She could only stare at her mother, her hands lying motionless upon the coverlet, while an instinctive terror crept through her body.

Senevia continued as though she were explaining a matter of ordinary household management.

“Why, do you suppose, is the water prepared for my bath invariably warm and sweetly perfumed? Why is my table furnished with every delicacy I desire? And why do the very persons who delight in tormenting you never dare to treat me in the same fashion? Shall Mother tell you the reason?”

She leaned closer until her lips were almost touching Talia's ear. The delicate fragrance of her perfume surrounded the child, bringing with it a painful familiarity, for it was the same scent which had once comforted her whenever she sought refuge in her mother's embrace.

“It is because they fear me,” Senevia murmured. “Some regard me with admiration, and a few, perhaps, with something approaching reverence. There are others, naturally, who despise me with all the bitterness of their hearts. Yet even those who most fervently wish me ill would never presume to make me the object of their petty cruelties.”

Her voice remained soft and composed, possessing the quiet certainty of one who had never doubted her own power.

“They may hate me, Talia, but they are obliged to exercise caution in my presence. They understand perfectly well that I am not a woman whom it is safe to offend. To them, I am someone to be feared, not someone to be tormented.”

She drew back sufficiently to look directly into her daughter's eyes. Beneath the exquisite serenity of her features, Talia perceived something dark and unfathomable, a quality which she had never before recognised in the mother she adored.

It was not merely confidence, nor even pride, but the terrible certainty of a woman who knew precisely how much power she possessed and had no scruples concerning its employment.

Senevia straightened her posture and delivered her final counsel with the calm authority of one imparting an essential lesson.

“Remember this well, my daughter. Those who possess both beauty and strength will inevitably become objects of envy and fear. But beauty without strength is a most dangerous possession, for it invites the covetousness of others while offering no defence against their cruelty.”

She paused briefly, allowing her words to settle before continuing.

“A beautiful creature who cannot protect herself will always be regarded as something to be seized and despoiled. Nowhere is this truth more mercilessly demonstrated than within the Imperial Palace. If you have no desire to be trampled beneath the feet of the many beasts who will one day seek to make you their prey, then you must at least possess the wisdom never to reveal yourself as weak.”

With these final words, Senevia turned away. She offered no embrace, no promise of protection, and no assurance that the servants responsible for Talia's suffering would be punished.

Her departure was accomplished with the same untroubled elegance with which she had entered, leaving behind a child whose desperate appeal for maternal comfort had been answered with a lesson in the necessity of inspiring fear.

That night, Talia lay awake beneath the coverlet, turning her mother's words over and over in her mind. The weak were trampled, Senevia had said, and those who possessed beauty without strength were destined to become the prey of others.

What frightened her most was not the possibility that her mother might have been correct. It was the discovery that Senevia had no intention of protecting her from the consequences of that truth.

Is this what a defeated soldier must feel when the last fortress upon which he depended has fallen?

The thought came to her with such dreadful clarity that she drew the coverlet more tightly around her trembling body. Until that afternoon, she had continued to believe that however cruel the world might become, there would always remain one person to whom she could appeal for refuge.

Now even that certainty had been taken from her.

If the servants grew bolder, if their punishments became more severe, or if they one day decided that mere humiliation was insufficient, who would prevent them from carrying out their wishes?

Certainly not Senevia. And as for the Emperor, Talia could scarcely imagine that a sovereign who had shown so little interest in his daughter would trouble himself over the sufferings of a child whose birth was regarded by so many as a stain upon his honour.

She curled herself into a tighter ball beneath the blankets and began biting her nails, an anxious habit which she had acquired during the lonely months of her confinement.

As she lay there, the memory of the dining room returned with such vividness that she almost imagined herself once more upon its cold floor. She saw the polished shoes of the servants moving around her, heard the quiet clatter of silverware being gathered, and remembered the dreadful indifference with which they had continued their duties while she lay trembling in her own sickness.

Those feet haunted her more terribly than the sight of the dead rat itself. They had passed so near her small, helpless body, yet not one person had paused to offer assistance.

It required very little imagination to picture those same feet descending upon her with deliberate force, crushing her beneath them as one might destroy some insignificant insect encountered upon a garden path.

Her eyes burned with unshed tears. Her mother had been right in one respect: those who appeared incapable of resistance invited the contempt of persons who delighted in exercising power over the defenceless.

If she continued as she was, shrinking beneath every hostile glance and accepting each indignity in silence, there would be nothing to prevent the servants from destroying what little remained of her dignity.

Yet as the hours passed, another thought began to emerge from beneath her terror.

Talia remembered the manner in which she had conducted herself before the incident in the secluded garden, when she had still believed that she possessed no reason to lower her eyes before anyone. She had been proud, wilful, and quick to protest against the slightest injustice.

The servants at the Taren estate had disliked her then, certainly, but they had not dared to torment her with the same unrestrained cruelty which she now endured.

The change had begun when she learned the circumstances surrounding Bernadette's death. Overwhelmed by the knowledge that her mother had caused such suffering, Talia had begun to regard herself as though she were somehow responsible for the grief of others.

She had lowered her head beneath their accusations, accepted their contempt without protest, and permitted herself to believe that perhaps she deserved a measure of punishment for the sorrow which accompanied her existence.

I have behaved as though I were guilty.

The realisation struck her with unexpected force.

She had committed no offence, yet she had carried herself like a condemned prisoner awaiting judgment. Every hesitant movement, every frightened glance, and every faltering word had proclaimed that she no longer possessed the courage to defend herself.

The servants, quick to recognise weakness, had responded accordingly. What had begun as petty indignities had grown into deliberate cruelty because they had discovered that their young mistress would neither resist nor demand that they answer for their conduct.

Talia lay motionless, considering this with an intensity quite foreign to her years. She could not alter the circumstances of her birth, restore Bernadette to life, or compel her half-siblings to regard her with affection.

Nor could she force Senevia to become the tender and protective mother she had once believed her to be. Those matters lay beyond her power, and perhaps they always would.

But the manner in which she faced the people who sought to humiliate her was something she might yet determine for herself.

The night advanced slowly, and the darkness beyond the curtains began at last to surrender to the pale light of approaching dawn. Talia had not slept, yet the terror which had seized her after her mother's departure gradually gave way to a strange and unfamiliar clarity.

She understood now that no one would come to rescue her merely because she was frightened, and that the privileges of an imperial princess were of little value unless she possessed the courage to assert them.

As the first light of morning entered the chamber, she lifted her head from the pillow and gazed towards the window. Her body remained painfully weak, and the memories of what she had suffered had lost none of their power to wound her.

Nevertheless, something within her had altered during those long hours of reflection.

And with the coming of dawn, Talia finally understood what she must do.
