THE FORGOTTEN FIELD · CHAPTER 03

The ballroom glittered beneath a thousand lights, and every polished surface reflected the magnificence of the imperial court. Ladies adorned in silk and precious stones gathered beneath the chandeliers, while gentlemen in embroidered coats exchanged pleasantries with the solemn importance of men who believed their smallest observations worthy of posterity.

Yet the evening’s most distinguished guests had not appeared.

Crown Prince Gareth, who ordinarily presided over such assemblies with the assurance of a man already accustomed to wearing the crown, was nowhere to be seen. Neither did his sister, Princess Aila, occupy her customary place beside him.

Talia regarded their absence with a satisfaction she made no effort to examine.

Perhaps the darling princess has at last discovered the virtue of remaining at home.

She accepted a goblet from a passing servant and turned it slowly between her fingers.

Only a few weeks had passed since Aila’s unfortunate collapse had provided the court with a spectacle far more memorable than any performance arranged for its entertainment. The princess, whose composure had so often been praised as the crowning ornament of the imperial family, had been reduced to a trembling creature before the astonished eyes of the nobility.

Talia remembered the confusion, the hurried footsteps, the servants summoned in alarm. Most vividly, she remembered the terrible hope that had taken possession of her.

How easily the world might have changed that night.

Had Aila never recovered, the court would have dressed itself in mourning. The priests would have spoken of heavenly innocence, the nobles of a flower cut down before its season, and Gareth would undoubtedly have regarded his sister’s death as a calamity from which the empire itself could scarcely recover.

And Talia? She would have been expected to weep.

A faint, bitter smile disturbed her lips.

What an accomplished actress they would have required me to become.

“Your Imperial Highness.”

The voice intruded upon her reflections with such unexpected intimacy that she turned sharply.

Before her stood a gentleman of agreeable appearance, dressed in dark green, whose immaculate attire suggested either considerable wealth or a devotion to personal vanity. His bow was faultless; his smile, rather less so.

She recognised him as one of the gentlemen who frequently attended her mother’s private entertainments.

“Have we met, sir?” she asked, although she knew perfectly well that they had.

“On several occasions, Your Highness. I am Count Serian. I fear I have made a lamentably small impression upon your memory.”

“Then you must endeavour to make a better one this evening.”

The Count laughed softly. “A challenge I should be delighted to accept.”

He offered his hand, and when she reluctantly extended hers, he bowed over it with a ceremony that seemed altogether too elaborate.

“You are looking exceptionally lovely tonight, Princess. Indeed, I scarcely know whether the court deserves so remarkable an ornament.”

“Your compliments are extravagant, Count.”

“Only because the truth demands extravagance.”

“Does my mother pay you to speak in such a fashion?”

His expression altered scarcely at all. “Her Majesty expressed a particular wish that you should not find the evening disagreeable.”

“How considerate of her.”

There was sufficient frost in those four words to silence a more perceptive gentleman. Count Serian, however, appeared entirely undisturbed.

“She was anxious that you should have pleasant company.”

“And you have been selected for the honour?”

“I volunteered with uncommon enthusiasm.”

“How noble.”

“Not noble, Princess. Merely selfish.”

She raised an eyebrow. “I should be interested to hear your explanation.”

He leaned a little nearer, lowering his voice beneath the music.

“A gentleman cannot stand in a room with the most striking lady present and remain content to admire her from the opposite wall. Yet there are gentlemen here who have resolved to do precisely that.”

“Out of modesty?”

“Out of fear.”

“Of whom?”

“Surely Your Highness need not ask.”

Talia followed his glance towards the elevated gallery reserved for the imperial family.

“Ah,” she murmured. “My affectionate brother.”

“The Crown Prince is not renowned for rewarding those who disregard his preferences.”

“And you are unafraid of his displeasure?”

“My dear Princess, a man who has already forfeited the Crown Prince’s affection has remarkably little left to lose.”

She regarded him more attentively. There was something peculiarly distasteful about a gentleman who could acknowledge his own disgrace with such cheerful indifference. Yet he possessed one advantage: he was willing to stand beside her when others preferred to keep their distance.

That, she decided, might be sufficient reason to tolerate him.

“Very well, Count. You may accompany me.”

He bowed once more. “Your generosity overwhelms me.”

“Pray do not mistake necessity for generosity.”

“I shall endeavour to remember the distinction.”

They moved towards the centre of the assembly.

“Tell me,” Talia said, “what instructions did Her Majesty provide? Was I to be rescued from solitude, or merely prevented from causing an embarrassment?”

“Her Majesty desired that you should enjoy yourself.”

“How astonishingly vague.”

“She also expressed the hope that no lady present should eclipse you.”

Talia turned her head. “Did she indeed?”

“Most emphatically.”

For the first time since his arrival, the Count had said something capable of engaging her interest. Before she could reply, he stopped and offered his hand.

“May I claim a dance, Your Highness?”

She looked at the waiting hand. The thought of submitting to the familiar liberties of a stranger filled her with reluctance. She disliked the careless confidence with which gentlemen presumed that a dance entitled them to touch a lady’s waist, guide her movements, and speak into her ear.

Yet she was conscious of the company watching. Some appeared curious. Others, disapproving. A few ladies had already begun whispering behind their fans.

They would prefer that I disappear.

That I should sit obediently in some neglected corner, grateful for the privilege of being admitted at all.

She lifted her chin.

Then they shall be disappointed.

“You may have the dance, Count.”

His face brightened. “Your Highness does me a singular honour.”

“See that you justify it.”

He led her among the dancers.

To Talia’s surprise, the Count proved an excellent partner. His movements were assured without being forceful, his timing impeccable, and his attention sufficiently disciplined that she was never obliged to correct a step or anticipate an awkward turn.

The music gathered around them. Silk swept across polished marble. Jewels flashed beneath the chandeliers. The musicians played with increasing spirit as the couples moved through the figures of the dance.

For a little while, Talia forgot to resent the hand supporting hers.

She had always taken pleasure in dancing, though the court had seldom allowed her to enjoy it without some accompanying humiliation. There were always comparisons to be made, always some observation concerning her birth, her manners, her resemblance to the Empress.

Tonight, however, the comparisons appeared to have ceased. Or rather, they had taken a different form.

The company was watching her. Not with the contemptuous curiosity she knew so well, but with something more complicated. Admiration, perhaps. Apprehension. Fascination.

As Count Serian guided her through another turn, she caught sight of a noblewoman who had once refused to acknowledge her greeting. The lady now stood perfectly still, her fan forgotten in her hand.

Talia smiled.

So you are capable of looking at me after all.

The music rose. For a moment, she imagined herself possessed of her mother’s extraordinary power: that dangerous, effortless authority by which Senevia could command a room without raising her voice.

She understood, suddenly, why the Empress never lowered her eyes. To be watched in such a manner was intoxicating.

And Talia, who had spent so much of her life being reminded of everything she was not, permitted herself the dangerous pleasure of believing that she might become something formidable.

Then the music stopped.

A hush moved across the ballroom. The assembled nobility turned towards the entrance with the instinctive obedience of flowers turning towards the sun.

The Crown Prince had arrived.

Gareth entered with the composure of a man for whom every door opened by divine appointment. At his side walked Princess Aila, dressed in white, her appearance so immaculate that one might have imagined the evening had been arranged for the express purpose of displaying her innocence.

Aila smiled. That smile was sufficient to destroy Talia’s brief happiness.

So she has recovered.

The thought came with such bitterness that Talia scarcely recognised it as her own.

Her sister looked radiant. There was no trace of the dreadful weakness that had once reduced her to helplessness before the court. Her complexion was clear, her bearing graceful, her expression composed.

Even the memory of her humiliation seemed to have been erased by the eagerness with which the company welcomed her.

Ladies curtsied. Gentlemen bowed. Everywhere there were expressions of delight and relief.

Talia watched in silence.

She knew the distinction between herself and her siblings had never depended upon beauty alone. Aila possessed something that no gown, jewel, or accomplished dance could bestow upon Talia.

Legitimacy.

Gareth and Aila belonged to the empire in a manner she never could. Their existence was celebrated as a blessing. Hers had been discussed as an offence.

Her mother had risen to a position of almost unimaginable influence, yet even Senevia’s triumph had not been sufficient to erase the circumstances that attended Talia’s birth.

The court could admire the Empress. It could fear her. It could flatter her, imitate her, and obey her. But it would never forget. And it would certainly never permit Talia to forget.

She tightened her hold upon her goblet.

Look at them.

How readily they bow. How readily they smile.

As though virtue were inherited with a name.

A gentleman standing nearby glanced from Aila to Talia, then whispered something to his companion. Both looked away when she noticed them.

The movement was insignificant. It was also unbearable.

Talia felt her earlier triumph dissolving into something dark and violent.

Do not measure me against her.

Another whisper. Another discreet glance.

Do not presume to judge which of us is worthy.

She turned away before her expression could betray her.

Count Serian, unfortunately, had been watching with considerable interest.

“An impressive entrance,” he observed.

Talia said nothing.

“Their Highnesses appear to have attracted the entire court.”

“How fortunate for them.”

“Indeed. Though I confess I find their conduct towards you rather curious.”

She looked at him sharply. “What conduct?”

“Why, they have not acknowledged you.”

“Perhaps they have not seen me.”

“Is that what Your Highness believes?”

His tone was light, but his eyes betrayed amusement. Talia’s face grew cold.

“You presume a great deal, Count.”

“Only that a family reunion might benefit from a little warmth.”

“Then I advise you to seek your warmth elsewhere.”

He smiled. “Shall I escort you to them?”

“No.”

“Not even to offer your congratulations upon the princess’s recovery?”

“Count.”

“Forgive me. I merely imagined you had come tonight with some particular intention.”

She turned fully towards him. “And what intention would that be?”

His smile widened. “To remind the court that the imperial family possesses another daughter.”

For several moments she could find no reply.

The Count had spoken with an insolence that ought to have offended her beyond forgiveness. Yet beneath the insult lay an observation she could not deny.

She had not come to the banquet in search of music, conversation, or amusement. She had come because the thought of Gareth and Aila receiving the adoration of the court without interruption was intolerable.

She wanted them to see her. She wanted them to remember her existence. Above all, she wanted to disturb that serene happiness which seemed to belong to them so naturally.

And somewhere beyond the ballroom, she knew her mother would be expecting no less.

Talia lowered her gaze to the wine trembling faintly in her goblet.

Very well.

If they insist upon pretending that I am invisible, I shall make myself impossible to overlook.

She drew herself upright. The Count stepped aside, apparently satisfied with the effect of his remarks.

“Your Highness?”

“I believe,” she said, “that I shall greet my family after all.”

“A most gracious decision.”

“Do not congratulate me too soon.”

She moved forward.

The crowd had gathered thickly around Gareth and Aila, but the nobles nearest Talia began to part as she approached. A few recognised her intention. Others appeared merely surprised that she should dare interrupt the royal reception.

She scarcely noticed them. Her attention remained fixed upon the two figures at the centre of the gathering.

One more step. Then another.

She could already imagine the moment when Gareth would turn and discover her standing before him. She could imagine Aila’s smile faltering.

The anticipation sent a fierce, almost pleasurable thrill through her.

Then something changed.

A murmur arose near the great doors. It travelled across the ballroom with extraordinary speed, interrupting conversations and drawing the attention of those who had only moments earlier been wholly occupied with the Crown Prince.

Talia stopped. At first, she did not understand why. Then she turned.

A gentleman had appeared at the entrance.

He stood apart from the brilliant confusion of the court with an imposing stillness that made the surrounding splendour seem suddenly insignificant.

Barkas Raedgo Siyeokan.

The name struck through Talia’s thoughts before anyone spoke it aloud.

Her fingers tightened around the goblet. The anger that had sustained her only a moment earlier vanished beneath an entirely different sensation.

She had imagined herself prepared for every humiliation the evening might offer. She had not prepared herself for him.

Why is he here?

Her breath caught. For one dreadful instant, she could neither advance nor retreat.

And as Barkas stood beneath the lights of the imperial ballroom, Talia felt the terrible certainty that the evening had ceased to belong to her intentions.

Something else had begun.
