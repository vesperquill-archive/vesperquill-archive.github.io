Chapter 6

Scarcely a fortnight had passed since Talia had departed the Taren estate to take up residence within the Imperial Palace, yet already she had begun to regard her former home with a longing she had never imagined herself capable of feeling.

Her mother, Senevia, had received the change in their circumstances with undisguised delight. She spoke endlessly of the honour now bestowed upon her daughter, of the recognition that would at last be granted to their family, and of the day when Talia's name would take its rightful place among the descendants of the imperial house. To Senevia, their arrival represented the fulfilment of ambitions she had cherished for many years.

To Talia, it represented little more than an exile.

She had expected grandeur, certainly. Her mother had described the palace in such extravagant terms that Talia had imagined halls filled with warmth and splendour, gardens more beautiful than those of any noble estate, and servants who would attend upon her with the deference owed to a princess.

The reality had proved considerably less agreeable.

The palace was magnificent, but its magnificence possessed a coldness that no quantity of gold or marble could disguise. Every corridor seemed to conceal a pair of disapproving eyes, and every encounter with the household servants reminded Talia that her presence was regarded as an unwelcome intrusion.

She had never before understood how thoroughly a person might be made to feel unwanted without anyone uttering a single openly insulting word.

At the Taren estate, even the sternest attendants had occasionally shown her kindness. Here, the servants obeyed her instructions with impeccable correctness, yet their manner was so distant that she sometimes wondered whether they would have preferred to pretend she did not exist at all.

Her mother, meanwhile, was occupied with matters of considerably greater importance—or so Senevia appeared to believe.

There were chambers to redecorate, furnishings to replace, and innumerable arrangements to be made before the palace could be rendered suitable to her tastes. Whenever Talia attempted to approach her, she was generally informed that Her Ladyship was engaged with the architects, the decorators, or some other person whose business could not possibly be interrupted.

Thus, although surrounded by more people than she had ever encountered in her life, Talia found herself quite alone.

She had begun to seek refuge in the quieter portions of the palace grounds, particularly those places where the servants had little reason to follow her. The rear garden of the Separate Palace soon became her favourite retreat.

It had once been a beautiful garden, or so she had been told. Great trees had shaded its winding paths, and flowerbeds had surrounded the fountains and ornamental pools. Now, however, the place bore the appearance of a battlefield from which the victorious army had only recently departed.

Senevia had ordered the grounds altered so extensively that scarcely anything remained of their former arrangement. Trees had been uprooted, shrubs removed, and the earth disturbed in preparation for new plantings. The more conspicuous portions of the palace gardens were already being restored, but the neglected grounds behind the Separate Palace remained a wilderness of broken soil and abandoned trenches.

Talia preferred them in that condition.

There were no courtiers to examine her clothing, no ladies to whisper behind their fans, and no attendants to remind her of the behaviour expected of an imperial princess. Among the overturned earth and empty flowerbeds, she could forget, if only for an hour, that she had become an object of such universal curiosity.

On the afternoon in question, she had escaped her apartments following a particularly disagreeable encounter with her nurse.

The woman had insisted upon arranging Talia's hair in a fashion which required an extraordinary amount of pulling, pinning, and scolding. Every protest had been met with a lecture concerning the duties of a young princess, until Talia had finally decided that even the cold rain outside would be preferable to another moment beneath the woman's merciless comb.

She had slipped away while the maid was occupied with the dressing table and made her way towards the rear garden.

Rain had been falling since midday. It descended in a steady, silvery curtain, softening the outlines of the palace walls and turning the newly disturbed earth into a dark and treacherous mire. The gardeners had abandoned their labours, leaving their tools beneath a wooden shelter, and not a single servant appeared among the deserted paths.

Talia crouched beneath the partial protection of a projecting wall and watched the raindrops gather upon the stones.

She could not have said how long she remained there.

Time passed strangely in the palace. The hours seemed endless when she was confined to her apartments, yet they disappeared without notice whenever she succeeded in escaping the attentions of her household.

She had nearly surrendered herself to a pleasant state of idleness when an unfamiliar sound disturbed the quiet.

It was faint and intermittent, scarcely distinguishable from the pattering rain.

Talia raised her head.

For a moment, she imagined that she had heard a whistle carried upon the wind. She listened again, but the sound did not return immediately.

Then it came once more.

She rose and followed it towards the farther boundary of the garden, where the remains of an enormous tree had been removed earlier that morning.

The tree had stood there for longer than anyone could remember. Its trunk had been so broad that several children might have joined hands around it without encircling it completely. Now the ancient giant was gone, leaving behind an ugly hollow surrounded by mounds of freshly excavated soil.

Talia approached the edge and peered down.

Something moved in the mud.

At first she mistook it for a fallen leaf, but the movement came again, accompanied by a thin, plaintive cry.

It was a bird.

A very small bird, scarcely more than a fledgling, lay trapped in the thick mud at the bottom of the hollow. Its feathers were soaked through, and its little wings struggled uselessly against the weight of the earth that clung to them.

Talia stared.

How did you come to be there?

She glanced towards the empty space where the great tree had stood.

Perhaps the bird had lived among its branches. Perhaps its nest had been destroyed when the gardeners removed the trunk. Whatever the explanation, the miserable creature had evidently been left to contend with circumstances for which nature had provided it no defence.

The rain continued to fall.

Each drop seemed enormous against the bird's frail body, and the little creature's efforts to free itself grew weaker with every passing moment.

Talia remained at the edge of the hollow, uncertain what she ought to do.

She was not especially fond of animals. Indeed, she had generally considered them troublesome creatures, inclined to scratch, bite, or soil whatever expensive garment happened to be nearest them.

Nevertheless, there was something distressing about the sight before her.

The bird was so small.

And there was no one to help it.

She looked around the deserted garden, hoping that a gardener or servant might appear. No one did.

After another moment of hesitation, she gathered her skirts and began to descend.

The attempt was disastrous.

The rain had transformed the earth into a substance far less dependable than it appeared. Her shoes sank immediately, and when she attempted to withdraw one foot, the other slipped beneath her.

She fell forward with a cry of indignation.

Cold mud splashed against her face and hands. Her beautiful green gown, newly made for her arrival at court, was stained almost beyond recognition, while the carefully arranged braids upon her head came loose and dragged through the wet earth.

For several moments, Talia could do nothing but stare at the ruin of her appearance.

Then she burst into an angry complaint.

“Of all the foolish things I might have done! I ought never to have come down here.”

She struggled upright, wiping the mud from her lips with the back of her hand.

“And for what? A miserable little bird that does not even belong to me!”

Her annoyance increased as she examined her gown. She could already imagine the expression upon her nurse's face when she returned to the palace, and the prospect of enduring another lecture was almost enough to make her abandon the entire undertaking.

She turned towards the slope.

Then the bird cried again.

The sound was so faint that it might easily have escaped her notice, yet something in it made her stop.

She looked back.

The little creature had ceased struggling. Its wings lay helplessly against the mud, and its head had fallen to one side.

For one dreadful moment, Talia believed it had died.

She hurried towards it, heedless now of the mud that clung to her shoes and skirts.

“Do not be dead,” she muttered. “I have made quite enough of a fool of myself already.”

Kneeling beside the bird, she reached down with both hands and lifted it from the mire.

It was lighter than she had expected.

Its body rested almost without movement against her palms, and its soaked feathers offered scarcely any protection from the cold.

Talia bent closer.

There was a heartbeat.

Faint, but unmistakable.

You are alive.

Relief passed through her with such suddenness that she scarcely understood it.

She cupped her hands around the little creature and breathed gently upon its feathers, hoping to restore some measure of warmth. After a moment, its beak moved, and one fragile wing stirred against her fingers.

“There now,” she whispered. “You need not struggle so terribly. I have you.”

The words surprised her.

She had not intended to speak kindly, and she was not accustomed to feeling such tenderness towards anything so small and helpless.

Yet as she looked down at the bird, she experienced an unfamiliar ache within her chest.

It had lost its home.

There was no mother to shelter it, no nest to which it might return, and no kindly hand waiting to rescue it from the rain.

Talia could not have explained why these circumstances troubled her so deeply.

She only knew that she could not bear to leave it there.

Carefully, she drew the bird against her neck, where the warmth of her skin might offer it some relief. It settled weakly against her, and she held it in place with one hand while examining the steep sides of the hollow.

Her difficulties, unfortunately, were far from over.

The descent had been foolish enough. The prospect of climbing out appeared considerably worse.

The rain had loosened the soil, and every attempt to gain a foothold caused the earth to crumble beneath her shoes. She tried several times to ascend, but each effort ended with her sliding back towards the bottom.

There was only one sensible course remaining.

She would have to crawl.

Talia regarded the slope with profound indignation.

An imperial princess, crawling through the mud like a farm animal! The very idea offended every principle of dignity her mother had laboured to instil in her.

Yet the bird could not be abandoned, and she could scarcely carry it safely while attempting to climb upon her hands and knees.

She considered calling for help.

But the garden was deserted, and she had wandered so far from the palace that it seemed unlikely anyone would hear her.

Thus she remained where she was, standing ankle-deep in the mud with the little bird sheltered against her throat.

The rain grew heavier.

Her gown clung uncomfortably to her legs, and the cold began to penetrate even the stubbornness upon which she ordinarily relied.

She did not know how long she had been waiting when a figure appeared beyond the curtain of rain.

At first she could distinguish little more than a tall, dark silhouette approaching through the mist. The stranger wore a hooded cloak, its heavy folds moving about him as he walked.

He stopped at the edge of the hollow.

Talia looked up.

Beneath the shadow of the hood, she saw a youthful face of remarkable beauty, its features composed with an almost unnatural severity. He appeared scarcely old enough to have exchanged childhood for adolescence, yet there was something in his bearing which made him seem older than his years.

Then she noticed his eyes.

They were pale blue, so clear and cold that they appeared almost luminous against the grey afternoon.

For reasons she could not understand, Talia found herself staring.

The stranger regarded her in silence for a moment before speaking.

“Have you some particular reason for remaining down there?”

His voice was quiet, but it possessed a chill which seemed altogether unsuited to so young a face.

Talia shivered.

At the time, she attributed the sensation to the rain.

Only much later would she remember that moment and wonder whether some part of her had recognised the danger long before her mind could comprehend it.

For the boy standing above her was Varkas Raedgo Sierkhan.

And the encounter which appeared so insignificant upon that rainy afternoon would become the beginning of an attachment from which she would one day find it impossible to free herself.

Had Talia possessed even the faintest knowledge of the sorrow awaiting her, she might have regarded the stranger's appearance very differently.

She might have endured the humiliation of crawling through the mud. She might have fled the garden without another glance towards the pale-eyed boy who had discovered her there.

Perhaps she would have persuaded herself to forget his face entirely.

But Talia was only eight years old.

She knew nothing of the cruelties affection could inflict, nor of the strange manner in which a single memory might come to govern the course of an entire life.

All she knew was that she was cold, thoroughly miserable, and exceedingly annoyed to be discovered in such an undignified condition.

Accordingly, she raised her chin and answered with all the sharpness she could summon.

“Is it not perfectly obvious? I have fallen into this dreadful hole, and I cannot get out.”

The boy regarded her for another moment.

His eyes moved briefly towards the muddy slope, then to the ruined gown and the small creature sheltered against her neck.

Talia expected a reprimand.

Indeed, his expression suggested that he was considering several questions concerning her judgment, none of which she particularly wished to answer.

But he said nothing.

Instead, he stepped over the edge.

Talia stared in astonishment as he descended into the hollow with a composure entirely at odds with the circumstances. His boots sank into the wet earth, and mud splashed against the fine fabric of his trousers, yet he displayed not the slightest concern for the damage.

It was an extraordinary thing to witness.

The boy looked as though he belonged among polished marble floors and silent, dignified chambers, not in a muddy excavation behind an abandoned garden.

And yet he came towards her without hesitation.

Up close, he appeared even taller than she had first imagined. His movements were controlled and deliberate, and there was something strangely reassuring about the certainty with which he crossed the treacherous ground.

Talia tightened her hold upon the little bird.

The boy stopped before her.

For a moment, he examined her face with those remarkable blue eyes. She could discover neither amusement nor irritation in his expression, and his silence made her unexpectedly nervous.

Then he extended his hand.

“Take my hand.”
