Chapter 8

In the weeks following that memorable afternoon, Talia acquired a habit which no amount of disappointment could persuade her to abandon. Whenever she could escape the watchful eyes of her attendants, she made her way towards the rear garden, lingering beneath the trees and wandering along the paths in the hope of encountering the extraordinary boy who had once rescued her from the mud.

Spring advanced, and the ruined garden gradually surrendered to the ambitions of the palace gardeners. A young elm was planted near the hollow where she had discovered the injured bird, while fresh beds of roses and flowering shrubs transformed the neglected grounds into a place of considerable beauty. The heaps of earth disappeared, the pathways were restored, and the last traces of the old garden were concealed beneath the splendour of its replacement.

Yet the boy did not return.

Talia searched for him with a persistence that would have astonished anyone who knew how readily she grew tired of other pursuits. She examined the faces of passing servants, observed the gentlemen who accompanied visiting nobles, and sometimes remained in the garden long after the evening shadows had begun to gather.

Her efforts were rewarded with nothing.

The memory of their encounter became increasingly precious to her. She remembered his dark cloak, his quiet manner, and the curious gentleness with which he had taken the helpless bird into his care. Above all, she remembered the warmth of his arms and the remarkable blue eyes in which she had imagined seeing little silver crowns.

There were evenings when she reproached herself for having obeyed her nurse so readily. Had she remained beneath the tree a little longer, she might have learned his name. Had she asked where he lived, she might have discovered how to find him again.

Perhaps she ought to have followed him.

The possibility occupied her thoughts with increasing frequency.

She had been so accustomed to demanding whatever she wished that it seemed almost absurd to have allowed something she desired so greatly to disappear without a protest.

And yet she had done precisely that.

Her disappointment was made more difficult to endure by the peculiar loneliness of her new life.

On the evening of the encounter, she had returned to her apartments expecting to be summoned before the Emperor. The anticipated meeting never occurred. Her father did not send for her, and Senevia, occupied with the endless affairs of the imperial household, failed to visit her daughter.

Talia ate her supper beneath the indifferent supervision of servants whose correctness of manner did little to conceal their lack of affection.

The meal was excellent, or so she supposed. She could remember nothing of its taste.

When at last she retired, she lay beneath the unfamiliar bedclothes and stared into the darkness.

The palace was very quiet at night.

Its great chambers, which appeared so magnificent beneath the daylight, became vast and unfriendly spaces after the lamps had been extinguished. Talia would draw the blankets about herself and imagine the comforting warmth of the stranger's embrace.

Sometimes she wondered whether the boy had truly existed.

It seemed impossible that so brief an encounter should remain so vivid in her memory when everything else about the palace had begun to blur into one long succession of disagreeable days.

Perhaps loneliness had endowed an ordinary act of kindness with an importance it did not deserve.

Perhaps she had imagined the tenderness in his manner.

Perhaps the boy himself had been little more than a pleasant dream.

By the time she had almost persuaded herself of this possibility, the seasons had changed.

Spring gave way to summer, and Talia passed her ninth birthday beneath a sky which seemed determined to pour all the heat of the heavens upon the imperial capital.

It was during this season that she saw him again.

The discovery occurred quite unexpectedly.

She had been summoned to the Emperor's private apartments and was proceeding along one of the long corridors of the Main Palace under the supervision of an elderly attendant.

The passage was cool and solemn, its polished floors reflecting the light that entered through a succession of tall arched windows. Talia walked beside her escort without enthusiasm, occupied chiefly by the disagreeable prospect of another meeting with a father who had shown so little inclination to know her.

Then a commanding shout rose from the grounds below.

She stopped.

Another shout followed, accompanied by the sharp sound of wood striking wood.

Curiosity overcame her reluctance, and she approached the nearest window.

Beyond the palace walls lay an extensive training ground, its pale earth dazzling beneath the summer sun. A company of young men, dressed in dark surcoats, were engaged in exercises with wooden swords under the direction of an instructor.

There must have been nearly thirty of them.

Talia scarcely noticed the others.

Her attention had settled upon one particular figure with such immediate certainty that she forgot to breathe.

It was the boy from the garden.

He wore no hood now, and the sunlight revealed hair of a pale golden colour, so light that it appeared almost silver where the rays fell most strongly upon it. His face was unmistakable, though the sternness she remembered seemed more pronounced beneath the severity of his training.

Talia leaned farther towards the window.

She had imagined this moment so often that she could scarcely believe it had arrived without warning.

There he was, moving among the other trainees as though he had never vanished from her life at all.

The boy possessed an extraordinary grace.

Where some of his companions appeared awkward beneath the weight of their weapons, he moved with an ease which made every action seem deliberate. His wooden sword rose and fell with remarkable precision, and even from the distant window Talia could distinguish the difference between his disciplined movements and the clumsier efforts of the others.

She watched him turn, advance, and withdraw.

There was something beautiful about the certainty of his movements, something which reminded her of the manner in which he had climbed the muddy slope while carrying her in his arms.

“Who is that gentleman?” she asked suddenly.

The elderly attendant followed her gaze.

“Those are the young noblemen presently receiving instruction for service among the Imperial Guards, Your Highness.”

“I can see that,” Talia replied impatiently. “I am asking about one of them.”

The attendant regarded the training ground with little interest.

“They are all from families of considerable standing.”

“But the fair-haired one—”

“His Majesty is expecting you, Princess.”

The rebuke was mild, but sufficiently firm to put an end to the conversation.

Talia cast one last look towards the boy before reluctantly withdrawing from the window.

She followed her escort through the remainder of the corridor, though her thoughts had been left behind upon the sunlit training ground.

For the first time since arriving at the Imperial Palace, she had discovered something that made the place seem less intolerable.

The Emperor's private chamber proved every bit as imposing as she had expected.

Its furnishings were magnificent, its walls adorned with costly works of art, and its windows so tall that the sunlight entered in broad golden columns. Yet none of these splendours offered the smallest comfort to the young princess who had been brought there.

Her father sat behind an enormous writing desk, occupied with a collection of official documents.

Talia paused near the entrance.

She had seen him before, certainly. During her childhood at the Taren estate, the Emperor had occasionally appeared in the company of her mother. Those visits had been treated as occasions of great importance, and the household had invariably been thrown into a state of agitation long before his arrival.

But Talia had never thought of him with the affection ordinarily associated with a father.

He was a distant and formidable gentleman who commanded her mother's attention and departed without appearing to remember that a child had been present.

She had sometimes resented him for that reason.

It was not merely that he failed to show her tenderness. He seemed to possess the power to remove Senevia from her life whenever he wished, leaving Talia to wait until her mother chose to return.

Now, although her name had been acknowledged among the descendants of the imperial house, the relationship between them remained as cold as ever.

The Emperor did not rise when she entered.

Nor did he greet her.

For several moments, he continued reading the document before him, apparently unaware that his daughter stood waiting across the room.

At length, he spoke.

“You will begin receiving the instruction appropriate to your station.”

Talia straightened.

He pressed his seal upon the parchment.

“The privileges of the imperial family are accompanied by obligations. You must become acquainted with the customs of the court and learn to conduct yourself accordingly.”

His tone suggested that he was delivering instructions to an official rather than addressing a child.

Talia waited for him to look at her.

He did not.

“I have arranged for suitable tutors to attend upon you,” he continued. “You will present yourself at the Main Palace each morning. Your education has been neglected in certain respects, and the deficiency must be corrected.”

Talia lowered her eyes.

She had imagined that he might ask whether she was comfortable in her new apartments, or whether she had grown accustomed to the palace. She had even wondered whether he might remark upon her resemblance to Senevia.

After all, she had frequently been told that she possessed her mother's eyes, her complexion, and much of her beauty.

Surely a man who professed such devotion to Senevia ought to find something pleasing in the face of her daughter.

But the Emperor showed no interest in examining that face.

He made another notation upon his papers and dismissed her with a movement of his hand.

The audience was concluded.

Talia stood motionless for a moment, uncertain whether she ought to speak.

Then she bowed as she had been taught and withdrew.

She had not received a single word of affection.

The disappointment was not particularly surprising.

Nevertheless, it left a small, disagreeable emptiness within her.

On her return through the corridor, she hurried towards the window overlooking the training ground.

The courtyard was deserted.

The exercises had ended, and the young knights had departed, leaving nothing behind but the white glare of the afternoon sun.

Talia rested her hands upon the windowsill.

For a moment, she felt almost as though she had lost the boy a second time.

But now she knew where to find him.

And that knowledge changed everything.

From that day forward, Talia's journeys to the Main Palace acquired a purpose which had little to do with her education.

Each morning, she contrived to pass the windows overlooking the training grounds. If the young knights were assembled, she slowed her steps and searched among them until she discovered the familiar figure with pale golden hair.

Sometimes she was fortunate enough to find him practising with a sword.

At other times, he was engaged in exercises beneath the direction of an instructor, his expression as composed and serious as she remembered.

Talia grew familiar with the smallest details of his appearance.

She observed the faint colour that rose to his cheeks during strenuous exercise, the way his hair caught the sunlight, and the quiet concentration with which he attended to every instruction.

She began to distinguish his movements from those of his companions even before she could clearly see his face.

The discovery pleased her enormously.

It seemed to establish a private understanding between them, though the boy remained entirely unaware of her presence.

At times, she would rest her chin upon her hands and address him silently.

Have you kept your promise? What became of the little bird? Did it grow strong enough to fly away?

She wondered whether he had carried it home, whether he had found some quiet place in which to shelter it, or whether it had eventually recovered and flown into the open sky.

The possibility that it might have died troubled her.

Yet she preferred to imagine it alive and healthy, perhaps perched upon the window of the boy's chamber or singing among the branches of a distant tree.

There were many things she wished to ask him.

She wanted to know his name.

She wanted to hear his voice again.

And, most particularly, she wished to discover whether those strange silver lights still shone within his blue eyes.

She had begun to suspect that no amount of watching from a distant window would satisfy her curiosity.

One afternoon, while she was supposed to be occupied with a lesson in imperial history, Talia found herself gazing once more towards the training ground.

Her book lay neglected upon the windowsill.

The distant sounds of the courtyard were far more interesting than the solemn accounts of emperors and their conquests.

She was so thoroughly absorbed that she failed to notice anyone approaching.

Only when a shadow fell across the open pages did she become aware that she was no longer alone.

Talia turned.

Her mother stood behind her.

For a moment, the child could only stare.

Nearly a fortnight had passed since she had last seen Senevia at any considerable length. Yet the woman before her appeared so magnificent that Talia almost doubted whether she was looking at the same mother who had once shared her meals and listened to her complaints at the Taren estate.

Senevia had dressed with extraordinary care.

Her gown was fashioned from costly fabrics, its delicate ornaments arranged to emphasise the dignity of her new position. Jewels shone at her throat and wrists, while her beautiful face possessed that remarkable perfection which had so often inspired admiration among the nobles who visited their former home.

Talia had always known her mother to be beautiful.

But there was something different about her now.

She seemed less like a woman whom a child might embrace and more like a splendid figure painted upon the walls of a royal gallery.

Even the elven mages who had sometimes visited the Taren family, with all their celebrated grace and otherworldly beauty, would have appeared ordinary beside her.

Senevia looked down at her daughter.

“What has so completely captured your attention?”

Talia started.

She had intended to greet her mother with affection. Indeed, during the preceding days she had imagined throwing herself into Senevia's arms and demanding to know why she had been so thoroughly neglected.

Yet the words would not come.

Instead, she stepped away from the window.

“Nothing of consequence, Mother.”

Senevia's gaze moved past her.

She glanced towards the courtyard below, and a faint smile appeared upon her lips.

“Ah,” she murmured. “I believe I understand.”

Talia's heart began to beat more quickly.

Her mother approached the window and regarded the trainees for a moment.

Then her attention settled upon the fair-haired boy.

“That young gentleman,” she said, “is the son of Duke Sierkhan.”

Talia looked up sharply.

She had known that the trainees belonged to distinguished families, but the name of Sierkhan carried an importance far beyond that of an ordinary noble house.

Even at nine years of age, she understood that the great ducal families possessed influence and authority which few others could rival.

The discovery filled her with astonishment.

The boy who had climbed into a muddy pit without a moment's hesitation was the son of one of the most powerful men in the Empire.

Senevia observed her daughter's expression with evident amusement.

“You are interested in him, are you not?”

Talia felt the colour rise to her cheeks.

“I was merely watching the training.”

“Were you indeed?”

Her mother's smile deepened.

There was something in it that made Talia uncomfortable, though she could not have explained why.

Senevia bent slightly towards her.

“Would it please you if that young gentleman were to become yours?”

Talia stared.

She did not know what such a question could mean.

The boy was not a doll, a horse, or some costly ornament which might be presented upon a birthday. He was a person, and one whose manner suggested that he would take considerable offence at being treated as anyone's possession.

Yet the possibility of having him near her, of speaking with him whenever she wished, was so wonderful that she could not immediately dismiss it.

Her silence was answer enough.

Senevia laughed softly.

“My darling child,” she said, touching Talia's cheek, “there are very few things in this world which cannot be obtained by those who possess sufficient power.”

She leaned closer and kissed her daughter.

“If you desire him so greatly, perhaps I shall arrange matters to your satisfaction.”

Talia's heart gave a sudden leap.

But the pleasure was short-lived.

Senevia straightened, and the softness of her expression altered.

“Such favours, however, must be earned.”

Talia looked at her uncertainly.

“A daughter who wishes to be indulged must first demonstrate that she deserves her mother's generosity.”

The words were spoken with perfect composure.

Yet Talia heard the warning concealed beneath them.

Her gaze fell upon the neglected history book.

She had been instructed to devote herself to her lessons, and she had instead spent the afternoon watching the knights.

Senevia's smile remained unchanged, but Talia felt suddenly certain that her mother knew everything.

She seized the book from the windowsill.

“I shall return to my studies,” she said quickly.

“An excellent decision.”

Talia did not wait for another remark.

Clutching the volume against her chest, she hurried from the window and along the corridor.

She could feel her mother's gaze following her.

It was an unpleasant sensation, as though something delicate and invisible had fastened itself to her back.

Only when she had turned the corner did she slow her steps.

Her heart was still beating rapidly.

She had missed Senevia terribly.

Every night, she had longed for the comfort of her mother's presence. She had imagined complaining about the tutors, the servants, and the dreary formality of the palace. She had intended to demand that Senevia spend more time with her.

Yet when the opportunity had finally arrived, she had done none of these things.

She had been afraid.

The realisation disturbed her more than she cared to admit.

The woman who had once been her closest companion now seemed almost a stranger.

That night, Talia lay awake long after the household had retired.

The curtains stirred faintly in the summer breeze, and the pale moonlight fell across the embroidered coverlet of her bed.

She turned from one side to the other, unable to find comfort.

Her thoughts returned again and again to the conversation with Senevia.

At the Taren estate, life had not always been pleasant. Talia had known that there were people who disapproved of her birth, and she had sometimes been made to feel the consequences of circumstances she was too young to understand.

But she had never been entirely alone.

Her mother had stood beside her.

Senevia had laughed with her, comforted her, and assured her that the opinions of others were of little consequence so long as they remained together.

There had been moments when they seemed less like mother and daughter than two companions united against an unfriendly world.

Talia had believed that nothing could alter that bond.

Now Senevia was Empress.

Her days belonged to the affairs of the court, her attention to the endless demands of imperial life, and her affection seemed to have become something which Talia must earn through obedience.

The palace had given her a title, magnificent rooms, costly gowns, and more servants than she could ever have required.

Yet it had taken away the one person whose presence had once made her feel secure.

Talia drew the blankets closer about herself.

She felt very small within the great bed.

The silence of the room seemed to press upon her, and she wished with all her heart that someone would come and sit beside her.

Someone who would not look at her with suspicion.

Someone who would not measure her worth according to her rank, her lessons, or her usefulness.

She wanted only to be held with kindness.

She wanted someone to look at her as though her existence were a source of happiness rather than inconvenience.

Her thoughts returned to the boy beneath the rain.

She remembered the certainty with which he had descended into the muddy hollow, the warmth of his arms, and the care with which he had sheltered the helpless bird.

He had known nothing of her position.

He had not bowed or flattered her.

Indeed, he had spoken to her with an insolence which ought to have offended her beyond forgiveness.

Yet he had helped her.

He had offered kindness without asking for anything in return.

Talia closed her eyes.

For a moment, she could almost feel the warmth of his cloak about her again.

She thought of the fair-haired trainee upon the sunlit training ground, of the silver lights within his blue eyes, and of the promise she had made herself to discover what had become of the little bird.

There was no reason to remain forever at a distance.

She knew where he could be found.

And if she wished to speak to him, she would have to do more than watch him through a window.

By the time sleep finally approached, Talia had made her decision.

She would find a way to meet Varkas Sierkhan again.
